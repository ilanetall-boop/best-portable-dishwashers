The best countertop dishwashers under $300

Three hundred dollars buys a genuine dishwasher: a sealed tub, a heated wash, two spray arms and a pump that empties itself into your sink. It does not buy capacity, certification, or a brand that will still answer the phone in 2031. Here is what each price step actually changes.

By Ilane Tall · Updated August 13, 2026 · Research-based.

Three hundred dollars is a strange ceiling. It is high enough to buy a real machine — not a gadget, a sealed tub that sprays hot water under pressure and pumps itself out — and low enough that the compromises are structural rather than cosmetic. Above the ceiling you buy certification, capacity and a support history. Below it you buy the wash, and pay for the rest in inconvenience.

We compared every portable dishwasher in our database listing under $300 and organised them by what each price step buys rather than by rank. Nobody shopping at $170 is weighing it against $290; they want to know whether the extra $120 changes anything they notice on a Tuesday night. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it buys a program list nobody uses.

There are three meaningful steps. Around $170 you get a working tank machine and nothing surplus. Around $250 you get a full-size tub, dual water sources and the first published decibel figures. Between $279 and $299 comes the interesting part of the shelf: the first ENERGY STAR certification, the first machines quoting real place settings instead of "30+ dishes", and the drying tricks that separate dry plates from damp ones. Skip to the table, or to what you give up.

How this page is put together

Everything a manufacturer says about capacity, noise and water use here is a claim rather than a measurement, and we flag it as such.

Under $300, you are mostly buying the same box

Line the spec sheets up and something obvious appears. The HAVA measures 16.9 by 16.8 by 18.1 inches. The NOVETE measures 16.9 by 16.8 by 18.1 inches. The Hermitlux measures 16.85 by 16.73 by 18.23. The EUHOMY measures 16.83 by 16.73 by 18.03. These are not competing designs; they are the same contract-manufactured shell — roughly seventeen inches square, eighteen tall, five-litre tank, front door — sold under whichever brand ordered the container.

That is not a scandal; it is why the category exists at this price. But it changes how you shop: you are choosing between control boards, program lists, tank sizes, drying strategies and the odds the brand still exists when a door seal fails. It also explains why the listings shout. When the hardware is common, the copy does the differentiating, which is how one machine advertises "8 programs" where its dimensional twin advertises six and the difference is that one counts the drying fan and the rinse. Read the cycle names, not the number.

So pick the band that matches how much you need to wash, then choose inside it on the three things that genuinely vary: tank versus faucet, how the machine dries, and how tall it stands against your wall cabinets. If you have not measured that gap, do it now — our guide to apartments, dorms and RVs explains why the eighteen inches above your counter disqualifies more kitchens than price does.

What you actually give up under $300

Certification, on almost everything. ENERGY STAR is the only independently verified specification in this category, and exactly one machine on our shortlist carries it under the ceiling. That does not make the others wasteful — a small tub washing five litres uses far less than a running tap — but every efficiency claim below $279 is the manufacturer marking its own homework.

Honest capacity units. "6 place settings" is a defined quantity: a plate, bowl, glass, cup, saucer and cutlery, six times over. "30+ dishes" is not a unit at all — a saucer is a dish and so is a dinner plate. When a machine sharing its exact external dimensions with a four-setting model advertises "30+ dishes" instead, the vaguer number is doing work. Convert to place settings before comparing, and where a listing refuses to, assume the lower figure.

Real drying. None of these has a heated drying element. They dry with residual heat, a ventilation fan, a long air cycle, or a door that opens itself — roughly in that order of effectiveness. Glass and ceramic will mostly be dry; plastic will be wet every time, regardless of price. Rinse aid is the cheapest upgrade any of them can take.

Decibel figures you can rely on. Where a number is published at all it is a manufacturer's claim, measured in the manufacturer's own conditions with no third-party audit. Two machines claiming 40 dB are not necessarily equally quiet.

Brand permanence. A countertop dishwasher is a pump, a heater, a door seal and a control board — all wear items — and several of these names did not exist five years ago. COMFEE' is Midea's value badge, with a real parts network behind it; the others are marketing entities of varying permanence sitting on shared hardware. Factor that into the gap between $250 and $290 rather than treating support as free.

The false economies

A machine too small to hold a meal. This is the expensive mistake. A dishwasher earns its counter space by absorbing the entire aftermath of dinner in one unattended run. A tub that takes half of it does not remove a chore, it splits one: the machine cycle, plus the pans and big plates and whatever did not fit — which is still you at the sink with a sponge. The pattern is predictable: two runs a night for a fortnight, then one, then it becomes a drying rack, then it is unplugged on the floor of a closet. If your household regularly generates more than four place settings at dinner, the smallest machine worth considering is the six-place model. Our piece on whether portable dishwashers are worth it works through that math.

The UV "steriliser" box. Below about $150 the results fill with cabinets that hold a few plates under an ultraviolet lamp with a warm-air fan and call themselves dishwashers. They are not. No pump, no spray arm, no detergent, no mechanical action — nothing is removed, only irradiated in place. A dishwasher cleans by throwing hot detergent solution at dishes under pressure. If a listing does not name a spray arm and a wash cycle, it is a warming drawer with a lamp in it.

Buying a tank you will never fill, or a fitting you cannot use. With a straight tap and a removable aerator, faucet mode is strictly better — no refilling, no five-litre limit — and paying a premium for a tank buys a feature you will use twice. With a pull-out sprayer the reverse holds, and a faucet-only machine like the six-place COMFEE' is unusable at any price. Establish which kitchen you have before you shortlist, not after.

When it is worth stepping just over $300

The ceiling is arbitrary and the shelf just above it is unusually good value. At about $310 the black six-place COMFEE' is the same ENERGY STAR machine as our band-three pick in a different finish, so the practical price of six place settings runs from $279 to $310 depending on colour and the day. Also at about $310, an AIRMSEN claims 40-plus items and 12-inch plates from a five-litre tank, with a claimed 72 hours of ventilated storage after the wash.

Two justify crossing the line outright. The HAVA with an integrated water softener at about $329 is the more interesting: hard water films glasses, coats the heating element and shortens the life of a small machine, and this is the only countertop model we have found that lets you set a softener level to match local water hardness. If your utility's hardness report is high, that single specification does more for your results than any program list under $300. The Midea six-place at about $340 is the COMFEE' format with delay start from 1 to 24 hours and a Power Wash, sold by the parent company of the badge.

Our rule of thumb: choosing between $250 and $290, spend the $40 — it buys capacity or drying, and you notice both daily. Choosing between $290 and $340, spend only for a specification you can name, like the softener or the delay start. And if the choice is between a $170 machine and nothing, the $170 machine is a rational way to find out whether the category suits you. Frequently asked questions

Is a countertop dishwasher under $300 actually worth buying?

Yes, if the machine is large enough for the way you cook. Under $300 you get the same washing mechanism a full-size dishwasher uses: hot water sprayed under pressure with detergent, hotter and more thorough than hands can manage. What you do not get is capacity, a heated drying element, or in most cases an ENERGY STAR certification. The buying mistake is not the price, it is choosing a tub too small to absorb one meal, because a machine that needs two runs per dinner stops being used.

How many dishes does a $250 countertop dishwasher really hold?

Most machines in that band quote a claimed 30-plus dishes, which is not a defined unit. Models sharing the same external dimensions that quote place settings instead state four, so four place settings is the realistic figure: roughly four dinner plates, four bowls, four cups and the cutlery. Plate diameter is the other limit, with most tubs capping out between 10 and 12 inches, and dishes usually have to be loaded at an angle to reach the upper figure.

Do any countertop dishwashers under $300 have ENERGY STAR certification?

Very few. On our shortlist the six-place COMFEE' at about $279 is the only machine under the ceiling carrying it, and certification becomes common only above $300. ENERGY STAR matters here mostly because it is the one specification verified by someone other than the manufacturer — capacity, noise and water-saving figures on these listings are all self-reported claims.

Will dishes come out dry in a cheap countertop dishwasher?

Glass and ceramic mostly will; plastic will not. None of these machines has a heated drying element, so they rely on residual heat, a ventilation fan, a long air cycle, or a door that opens automatically at the end of the wash to let steam escape. The auto-open approach dries best, ventilation modes come second, and using rinse aid makes a bigger difference at this price than any program setting.
