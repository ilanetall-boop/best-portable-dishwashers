Comparison · Forno vs Sharp, 18-inch class

Forno and Sharp also make 18-inch built-in-style portable dishwashers.

Every roundup in this category defaults to the same three names — SPT, COMFEE' and Honeywell. Forno's Polo and Sharp's SDW4523MS sit a class above on price, and on paper, on the two specs that are hardest to fake: how quiet the machine runs and how it decides whether your dishes are actually clean.

Search "18 inch portable dishwasher" and the first page is almost entirely SPT, COMFEE' and a cluster of white-label Chinese-assembled brands, with Honeywell's 8-place-setting model usually sitting near the top as the value default at $339.98. What that search mostly buries is that two appliance brands most people already associate with kitchens — Forno, known for ranges and hoods, and Sharp, the Japanese electronics and appliance maker — also sell 18-inch, built-in-style portable dishwashers. Neither shows up often because neither is optimized to win an Amazon search on price; both are optimized to win on the two things a cheap machine usually can't claim: noise and wash intelligence.

This is a three-way comparison of the Honeywell 18-Inch Dishwasher (B0F9B9Q3BW, $339.98), the Polo 18" Dishwasher from Forno (B0FTS4453K, $545.00) and the Sharp SDW4523MS (B0FHS43RZ5, $699.00). All three are the 18-inch, built-in-style class — a real dishwasher-shaped appliance rather than a countertop tub — and all three are described by their listings as portable-capable within that class. The question this page answers is narrow and practical: is a known appliance-brand name worth $100 to $250 more here, and if so, for whom.

The gap

Why appliance brands barely show up in this category

The 18-inch portable class exists because a shopper's kitchen lacks something a full-size 24-inch built-in needs — plumbing, cabinet space, or a landlord's permission. That buyer is usually price-sensitive by definition: they're solving a plumbing or rental problem, not shopping for a flagship appliance. SPT, COMFEE' and Honeywell built their listings around that buyer, which is why Honeywell's 18-inch model undercuts both alternatives here by $200 or more.

Forno and Sharp sell into a different, smaller intent: someone who wants the 18-inch footprint but refuses to accept a budget-appliance feel — someone furnishing a casita, a basement kitchen, or a small-footprint primary kitchen where a visibly cheap appliance would stand out. That's a thinner slice of the search traffic, so neither brand's listing is built to rank on "cheap portable dishwasher," and most roundups that are themselves built around top-of-search products never surface them. The products exist; the category's dominant search intent just isn't the one they're built for.

Brand recognition inside the category also cuts the other way. SPT and COMFEE' are known specifically as compact-appliance specialists — shoppers in this category trust them because that's all they make. Forno is known for ranges, and Sharp for electronics and microwaves; neither name carries the same category-specific trust here that it would in its home category, even though both brands' general reputation for build quality is arguably stronger. The name recognition exists, it's just pointed at the wrong shelf.

Wash intelligence

Soil sensing on the Forno versus smart sensors on the Sharp

Both premium machines advertise a form of automatic wash-cycle adjustment that neither the Honeywell listing nor most budget 18-inch machines claims. The distinction in their own listing language is worth separating rather than treating as the same feature under two names.

The Polo's listing describes soil sensing: the machine reads how dirty the incoming water or the load appears to be and adjusts the cycle accordingly, a feature set that targets consistency across loads of varying griminess without the user picking a cycle by guesswork. The Sharp SDW4523MS's listing describes smart sensors more broadly, as part of a six-wash-cycle program that spans the Sharp's third-rack-equipped 8-place-setting layout. Both are manufacturer-stated features we have no independent measurement for, and neither listing publishes the sensor's underlying mechanism in enough detail to say definitively whether "soil sensing" and "smart sensors" describe identical hardware or different approaches to the same problem — turbidity sensing is the common industry method, but we're not asserting that's what either machine uses without it being stated.

What we can say concretely: Honeywell's listing makes no sensing claim at all. Its 6-washing-program lineup is fixed-cycle, which means the user picks the program and the machine runs it regardless of how soiled the load actually is. For a household that runs identical, moderately dirty loads every time, that's not a real disadvantage. For one that alternates between a light rinse-and-reload and a post-dinner-party full load, an automatic-adjusting cycle is the kind of feature that's invisible until the one night it saves a rewash.

Noise

47 dB to 52 dB: the quietest of the three, and what that's worth

Only two of these three listings publish a decibel figure at all, which is itself informative. The Sharp SDW4523MS states 47 dB, the Polo's listing title states 52 dBa, and Honeywell's listing makes no noise claim. Treat both figures as manufacturer claims measured in undisclosed conditions, not independently audited numbers — but the 5 dB gap between the two brands that do publish one is consistent enough across appliance categories to be worth taking seriously as a relative ranking, even if the absolute numbers aren't verifiable.

Five decibels doesn't sound like much until you remember the scale is logarithmic: roughly every 10 dB is a doubling of perceived loudness, so a 5 dB gap is a real, audible difference, not a rounding error. In a kitchen that's open to a living room or a studio apartment where the dishwasher runs while someone's on a call six feet away, that gap is the entire argument for buying the Sharp over the Polo, regardless of anything else either machine does.

Where this matters least: a closed kitchen, a run-it-overnight habit, or a household that's never once complained about an existing dishwasher's noise. In those cases, 47 versus 52 dB is a spec sheet detail, not a lived experience, and the $150-plus price gap between the two is better spent elsewhere.

Loading space

Third rack and adjustable upper rack: the extra loading space compared

All three machines claim 8 place settings, so the headline capacity number is identical across the comparison — the difference is in how that capacity is organized, and here the listings diverge more than the place-setting count suggests.

The Sharp SDW4523MS's listing specifically calls out a third rack, the thin sliding tray that sits above the main upper rack in modern full-size dishwashers and is built for flatware, small utensils and oddly-shaped items that otherwise fight for space in a cutlery basket. A third rack is a feature that's become close to standard on mid-range 24-inch built-ins over the past several years but is unusual to see claimed on an 18-inch portable-class listing — it's the single spec on this page that most clearly borrows from Sharp's full-size dishwasher lineup rather than from the compact-appliance category.

The Honeywell listing does not claim a third rack, but its listing bullets note an 8-place-setting stainless steel tub without further rack-configuration detail. Neither the Honeywell nor the Polo listing claims an adjustable upper rack in the specification lines available to us — where we don't have a stated spec, we're not asserting one. If rack flexibility — fitting a tall stockpot, or reconfiguring for a run of oversized mixing bowls — matters to how you actually load a dishwasher, the Sharp's stated third rack is the one concrete, checkable advantage in this category on this page; confirm current rack configuration on the live listing before ordering, since manufacturers do revise these between model years.

The decision

Is a known appliance-brand name worth $100-250 more here?

Framed as a brand question, the honest answer is no — brand recognition alone isn't the thing you're paying for with either Forno or Sharp, because neither name carries deep category-specific history in compact dishwashers the way SPT and COMFEE' do. Framed as a specification question, the answer is more interesting: you're paying for soil sensing, a lower stated decibel figure, and on the Sharp specifically, a third rack. Those are real, checkable features that the Honeywell's listing simply doesn't claim.

So the decision reduces to whether those three specific things are worth money to you, not whether "Sharp" or "Forno" on the door is. If your kitchen is open-plan, if you load oddly-shaped flatware every day, or if you've been annoyed by a previous dishwasher's noise, the $150.02 jump from the Polo to the Sharp — or the full $359.02 jump from the Honeywell to the Sharp — buys concrete, stated improvements. If none of that describes your situation, the Honeywell's $339.98 and 8 place settings do the same basic job the other two do, for less than half the price of the Sharp.

One more honest caveat: we have no verified star rating or review count for any of the three ASINs in our database, so none of this is "buyers love it" — it's "the spec sheet says this, and the price says that." FAQ

Common questions about Forno, Sharp and the 18-inch class

Is the Forno Polo the same machine as a Honeywell or SPT 18-inch portable with a different badge?

No, based on the listings we have. The Polo's listing claims soil sensing and a 52 dBa noise rating, neither of which the Honeywell listing claims, and front controls are called out specifically. We can't compare internal chassis or components beyond what each listing states, but the stated feature sets differ enough that they aren't being marketed as identical hardware.

Does a third rack actually add capacity, or just reorganize the same 8 place settings?

Based on the Sharp SDW4523MS's listing, it's the latter: the machine is still rated at 8 place settings with the third rack included, so the rack is about where items sit rather than how many you can wash. Its value is fitting flatware and small utensils that otherwise crowd a cutlery basket or sit awkwardly on a lower rack, not raising the total load size.

Why doesn't the Honeywell listing state a decibel rating?

We don't know Honeywell's reason, and we're not going to guess at marketing strategy. What we can say is that the absence of a stated figure means there's no manufacturer noise claim to compare against the Sharp's 47 dB or the Polo's 52 dBa — treat the Honeywell as an unknown on this spec rather than assuming it's louder or quieter than either.

Are any of these three machines rated higher than the others by verified buyers?

We don't know. None of the three ASINs — Honeywell B0F9B9Q3BW, Forno Polo B0FTS4453K, or Sharp SDW4523MS B0FHS43RZ5 — carries a verified rating or review count in our product database, so we have no review data to compare and we won't invent or paraphrase sentiment we don't have.
