Buying guide · Convertible 18-inch dishwashers

Freestanding now. Built-in later.

A small group of 18-inch dishwashers is listed under "Built-in" rather than "Portable," yet they turn up in portable shortlists anyway, because the same chassis can run freestanding on the day it arrives and be installed permanently under a counter whenever you're ready. The listing title rarely spells out which parts of that story apply to the exact unit you're about to buy.

"Convertible" is not a word Amazon lets manufacturers put in a dishwasher title, so it shows up as "Built-in Dishwasher" on a listing that also, further down the bullet points, describes a top that finishes the unit and lets it stand on its own in the middle of a kitchen. That is the whole category in one sentence: a machine engineered to be installed under a counter, sold in a configuration that doesn't require a counter at all, yet to a buyer scanning search results it looks identical to every other 18-inch portable dishwasher on wheels.

The distinction matters because the two product lines solve different problems. A dedicated portable dishwasher — the kind compared in our countertop-vs-18-inch breakdown — is built to roll to the sink and back for its entire working life; conversion was never part of the plan. A convertible model is the opposite default: it's a built-in dishwasher first, and freestanding is a documented accessory state rather than the end goal. The definition

What "convertible" actually means on a listing

Every dishwasher in this category is built on a standard 18-inch built-in chassis — the same width, the same internal tub and wash system a kitchen-cabinet dishwasher uses. What makes it convertible is that the manufacturer also ships, or sells separately, a finished top panel and a set of side panels that turn that chassis into a self-contained appliance able to stand in open floor space: no cabinet, no cut countertop, no permanent water line.

That is the entire mechanism. There's no second motor, no different wash system, no feature that a straight built-in model lacks. The conversion kit is cosmetic and structural rather than functional — it covers the sides that would otherwise be hidden inside a cabinet, caps the top with a surface you can set things on, and in most designs on the market adds casters so the finished unit can be rolled rather than carried. Removing that kit later is what exposes the raw built-in chassis again, ready for cabinetry.

The catch for a buyer is that listing titles describe the chassis, not the kit. All four models below are titled "Built-in Dishwasher," which is accurate, but it tells you nothing about whether a portable top-and-side-panel kit ships in the box, is sold as an accessory, or needs to be sourced separately. That detail lives in the full listing description, not the title — check it against the general expectations in what comes in the box with a portable dishwasher before assuming parity with a dedicated portable model.

Day one

Running it freestanding: the panel and finish it ships with

When a convertible 18-inch model is set up freestanding, the finished top panel is doing more work than it looks like. It's the surface that keeps the machine from looking like an unfinished cabinet component sitting in your kitchen, it's usually the only flat work surface the unit adds rather than consumes, and on most designs in this class it's also where the casters are mounted — which is the only thing making "roll it to the sink" possible with an appliance this heavy. Without that kit the chassis is just a built-in dishwasher with no way to stand safely away from a cabinet opening.

Finish matters here in a way it doesn't on a committed built-in: a panel that will spend its whole life facing outward needs to look finished from every visible side, not just the front door most built-ins show. Two of the listings below — both titled identically as a COMFEE' 18-inch Built-in Dishwasher — carry a roughly $40 price gap with no stated reason, which is exactly the kind of thing a finish or kit variant explains and a title never will. The practical trade-off of staying freestanding indefinitely is the same one that applies to any 18-inch dishwasher on wheels: it still needs a hookup at the faucet, it still occupies floor space permanently, and it still has to travel to the sink and back unless left parked beside it. None of that changes whether the chassis underneath happens to be convertible — a dedicated portable model never has to answer "does this look acceptable from four sides," because it was designed for that from the start, while a converted built-in is wearing an accessory, not its native form. Day two hundred

What a later built-in conversion requires, and what it doesn't

Converting one of these machines to a permanent built-in install is mechanically simple and logistically not. Simple, because the chassis was a built-in dishwasher the whole time: the panels and casters come off, the water supply and drain connections move from the temporary faucet hookup to a permanent line under the sink, and the unit slides into an 18-inch cabinet opening exactly as any built-in model would. Nothing about the wash system, the tub or the controls needs to change — the conversion is about plumbing and cabinetry, not the appliance itself.

What it doesn't require is a new dishwasher — that's the financial case for buying convertible rather than a dedicated portable model and a separate built-in later: one purchase, two installation states over its life. It also doesn't require the manufacturer to do anything at the point of conversion — no activation step, no firmware change. It's a physical reconfiguration a homeowner, handyman or plumber performs once.

What it does require, every time, is exactly what any built-in dishwasher installation requires: a dedicated or convertible under-sink water line with shutoff, a drain connection (typically tied into the sink's drain or disposal), an electrical connection appropriate to the unit, and — this is the part a rental kitchen usually can't offer — a cabinet opening sized for an 18-inch appliance, with the cabinet door or false front removed. None of the four listings below states whether a dedicated installation kit (hoses, brackets, the anti-tip bracket a permanent install needs) ships in the box or has to be bought separately, so that is a question for the specific listing and not something general industry practice lets us assume either way.

The other open question per model is cabinetry compatibility — whether the unit needs a full-height opening or can be set into a shortened one, and whether a trim kit ships with it. Titles don't carry that detail. Confirm it against the listing's installation instructions, not the marketing bullets, before assuming a cabinet you already have will take the machine unmodified.

If you're renting

Renters: why staying freestanding is usually the right call anyway

The ability to convert to a built-in is a real feature, and for most renters it's also one they'll never use — not because the machine can't do it, but because the two things a conversion needs most are exactly what a lease doesn't grant. Cutting a cabinet opening, running a permanent water line and wiring in a dedicated electrical connection are modifications to the unit, and most leases require landlord approval for exactly that or a return to original condition at move-out. A freestanding appliance that unplugs and rolls away requires neither.

There's a second, quieter reason to stay freestanding: a convertible model, in its freestanding state, is everything a dedicated portable 18-inch dishwasher is — faucet-connected, floor-standing, needing a clear path to the sink, as covered in our comparison of countertop and 18-inch portable classes. None of those floor-space or faucet-compatibility considerations disappear just because the chassis underneath happens to be convertible. If your kitchen already rules out a rolling 18-inch machine — no floor space, a sprayer faucet with no removable aerator — buying a convertible model doesn't fix that; conversion only ever addresses permanence, not fit.

For a household moving every one to three years, the honest calculation is that the appliance will likely move to the next rental still freestanding, long before a conversion is worth the plumbing work. That's not a strike against buying convertible — it's one purchase that works today and keeps a future option open. It's simply a reason not to pay extra for conversion-specific hardware you have no near-term plan to use. If you own the kitchen

Owners: what to check before paying someone to install it permanently

If you own the home and the conversion is genuinely on the table, the checklist to work through before calling a plumber or handyman is short but non-negotiable, because every item on it is a reason the job either goes smoothly or doesn't:

  • Confirm an installation kit exists or is sold separately. None of the four listings above states this plainly. A built-in install typically needs a water-supply line with shutoff valve, a drain connection, an anti-tip bracket and often a trim kit — ask the seller directly, in writing, before assuming it's in the box.
  • Measure the cabinet opening against the unit's built-in dimensions, not its freestanding footprint with panels attached. The panels add to the freestanding width and depth; the raw chassis that goes into a cabinet is narrower.
  • Check the existing plumbing and electrical. A dishwasher needs a drain line (often tied into a garbage disposal or the sink's trap), a hot water supply and a grounded circuit — a kitchen converted from an all-freestanding setup may have none of those readily available under the sink.
  • Ask what happens to the warranty. Warranties are typically tied to proper installation per the included manual; a self-converted unit installed outside the documented procedure can complicate a claim later.
  • Get a fixed labor quote, not just a parts estimate. A plumber pricing this job needs to see the actual unit and cabinet to quote accurately — the panels-off, plumb-in job is comparable to a standard built-in install, not a five-minute swap.

None of this is a reason to avoid the conversion if your kitchen genuinely has the cabinet space and plumbing access — it's a reason to treat "it's convertible" as the start of a short project plan, not a finished feature you flip on. FAQ

Common questions about convertible dishwashers

What does "convertible" mean on an 18-inch dishwasher listing?

It means the appliance is built on a standard 18-inch built-in dishwasher chassis, but can also be finished with a top panel and side panels — and in most designs, casters — that let it stand freestanding in open floor space instead of inside a cabinet. Removing those panels later and plumbing it in permanently converts it to a conventional built-in install. The wash system itself doesn't change between the two states.

Can I keep one of these freestanding forever and never convert it?

Yes. Nothing about the machine requires conversion, and for a renter especially, staying freestanding for the appliance's entire life is usually the more sensible choice — a built-in install needs a cabinet opening, a permanent water line and often a landlord's approval, none of which a freestanding setup requires.

What does converting one to built-in actually require?

Removing the top and side panels and casters, then installing the unit the way any 18-inch built-in dishwasher is installed: a water-supply line with shutoff, a drain connection, an appropriate electrical connection, and a cabinet opening sized for the unit with its door or false front removed. None of the listings in this guide states whether a dedicated installation kit ships in the box, so confirm that with the seller before assuming the parts are included.

Do I need a plumber to convert one to built-in?

Most owners hire a plumber, appliance installer or handyman for the water and drain connections, since incorrect plumbing can cause leaks or drain backups. The panel removal itself is straightforward, but running or modifying a permanent water line is the part of the job where professional installation is worth the cost for most households.
