Buying Guide · 18-Inch Portable Dishwashers

Full-size washing, no plumber required.

An 18-inch portable dishwasher is a real dishwasher on castors — eight place settings, a finished top, a hose that clips onto the kitchen faucet. It's the machine to buy when hand-washing has worn you down and installing a built-in isn't yours to decide. Here's what it buys you over a countertop unit, what it costs to live with, and when to just wait.

"Portable dishwasher" covers two unrelated machines. One is a countertop box the size of a microwave. The other is what this guide is about: a genuine 18-inch dishwasher standing on its own castors, built to roll to the sink, hook onto the faucet, run a full cycle, and roll back against the wall when it's done. It is the closest thing to a built-in dishwasher you can own without a plumber, a cabinet cut-out or a landlord's permission.

Why 18-inch

What an 18-inch machine buys over a countertop unit

A countertop dishwasher is a sealed tub that sits on your worktop, typically claiming four to six place settings from a tub 16 to 22 inches wide. An 18-inch portable is a different class of appliance entirely: a full-height, floor-standing machine with a finished top surface, built around eight place settings — close to what a standard 24-inch built-in holds. The SPT SD-9263W and its stainless twin, the SD-9263SSC, both list eight place settings and six programmes: Heavy, Normal, Eco, Glass, 90 Min and Rapid. The BLACK+DECKER BPD8B and the Hamilton Beach models on wheels list the same eight-setting capacity.

That capacity gap is the entire case for this machine. Four to six settings means planning meals around when the dishwasher last ran; eight settings is close to a full day's dishes for a family, run once. You also get back the worktop a countertop machine permanently occupies — an 18-inch portable lives on the floor and gives you a finished top surface in return, which several listings lead with rather than treat as an afterthought.

None of it comes free. Every 18-inch machine in our database is faucet-only — there is no tank version of this class, so a kitchen with no usable tap rules the category out entirely, which our countertop-vs-18-inch comparison covers in more depth. And the price roughly doubles: countertop machines in our data run $170 to $330, while the machines below run $585 to $632.

Living with it

Wheels, weight, and moving it to the sink twice a day

"Portable" is doing careful work in this category's name. These machines roll — they do not carry. An 18-inch dishwasher on castors weighs on the order of a large suitcase packed for a month, and it moves the way a heavy appliance moves: fine across a level kitchen or vinyl floor, awkward over a threshold, and a two-person job up or down a step. Budget an 18-to-19-inch strip of floor for it to park in, plus room for the door to swing open, plus a clear, flat path from that spot to the sink.

The daily routine is the same across every model here: roll it to the sink, unscrew the faucet's aerator, clip on the supplied adapter, connect the machine's hose, run the cycle, disconnect, roll it back. Hamilton Beach's listing calls this out directly — "easy to connect to any kitchen tap" — because the connection is a single quick-connect coupler carrying both supply and drain, not two separate hoses. It's a two-to-three-minute job once you've done it a few times, but it is a job, twice if you run the machine in the morning and again at night, and it means the sink is occupied and partly blocked for the length of the cycle.

Two things decide whether that routine is livable. First, your faucet needs a removable aerator and standard threads — pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets, now common in newer kitchens and rentals, generally will not accept the adapter, because the spray head itself unscrews from a hose rather than a fixed spout. Confirm this before you order; it is the single most common reason one of these machines goes back. Second, castors roll acceptably on tile, vinyl and sealed hardwood, and badly on thick rugs, uneven old floors, or a raised threshold between kitchen and hallway. If your kitchen floor isn't flat and hard, budget extra effort into the twice-daily roll.

Capacity

Eight place settings, and what that means for a family

A place setting is a defined unit, not marketing copy: one dinner plate, one side plate or bowl, one glass, one cup and saucer, and one person's cutlery — roughly ten items per setting. Eight settings, the figure SPT, BLACK+DECKER and Hamilton Beach all quote for their 18-inch machines, works out to around eighty pieces, which is close to what a standard built-in dishwasher holds and is generally enough for a family of four to run once a day rather than twice.

Two caveats apply to every number in this section, because they're manufacturer claims rather than independent measurements. First, they're produced with standardised, evenly stacked crockery, not your actual mismatched bowls and oddly shaped mugs — real-world capacity typically lands a little under the stated figure. Second, place-setting count says nothing about plate diameter, and none of the four machines below publish a maximum plate size in the data we have; if a large dinner plate matters to you, check the current Amazon listing before ordering rather than assuming it fits.

Eight settings is also the point where cycle choice starts to matter more than in a smaller machine, simply because a full 18-inch tub takes longer to clean thoroughly than a half-full countertop tub. All four machines here carry a Heavy or equivalent program, an Eco setting for a lighter, water-saving run, a Glass cycle, and a fast option for when you need clean plates in under an hour rather than a full two.

Finish and noise

Finish is cosmetic; noise is a spec sheet gap

Across these five machines the meaningful differences are narrower than the product photos suggest. The two SPT units are the same platform in white or stainless steel; the two Hamilton Beach units are the same line in white or stainless/platinum. Finish is a genuine decision if the machine will stand in view of your cooking and eating space, and close to irrelevant if it's parked against a wall or in a utility nook. The one machine that differs on more than color is the BLACK+DECKER, with its usable top surface, and the Hamilton Beach at 19 inches wide, which is worth checking against your floor space before you assume every listing here is interchangeable.

None of the five listings in our database publish an independent decibel rating, and where a manufacturer doesn't quote a number, we don't invent one. What we can say from the category generally: floor-standing 18-inch machines run at floor level, partially shielded by the counter overhang and surrounding cabinetry, which in our judgement tends to feel less intrusive in an open room than a countertop machine running at ear height three feet away — even when the floor machine's motor and pump are doing more work on a fuller load. If noise while the machine runs matters to you, check the specific listing for a decibel figure before you buy rather than assuming quiet.

When it's idle

Storage when it is not in use

Unlike a countertop machine, which lives permanently on your worktop, an 18-inch portable is designed to be parked somewhere between uses rather than tucked away. Most owners settle on one fixed spot — against a wall, in a corner, or backed into the gap where a built-in dishwasher would normally sit — and roll it out to the sink only when it's time to run a cycle. Because it stands on castors rather than sitting flush in a cabinet, that spot needs to be a flat, hard floor area roughly the machine's own footprint, plus enough clearance for the door to swing open on wash day.

The BLACK+DECKER's usable top surface is the clearest example of a machine designed with storage in mind: parked against the wall, it functions as a small counter rather than dead space, which is the whole argument for choosing it over a cheaper equivalent. Whichever machine you buy, plan its parking spot before it arrives — a family that hasn't measured a permanent spot for an 18-inch machine on wheels usually ends up leaving it wherever it was last used, which defeats the point of buying a machine you were told would roll out of the way.

The honest alternative

When you should just wait and install a built-in

An 18-inch portable exists to solve a specific, temporary problem: you want a real dishwasher and you are not currently able to install one — renting, between leases, or waiting on a kitchen renovation. It is not the cheaper or more convenient option once that constraint is gone. A built-in dishwasher plumbs in permanently, drains without a hose over the sink edge, and never needs rolling anywhere, and once you own the cabinet space and the plumbing, a comparable built-in typically costs less than $632 — the price of our most expensive pick here — while asking nothing of you twice a day.

Wait and install a built-in instead if any of the following is true: you own the kitchen or have firm permission and budget to modify it; you're annoyed enough by the daily connect-and-disconnect routine that you'd pay to never do it again; your floor space is tight enough that an 18-inch footprint is a genuine daily obstacle rather than a minor inconvenience; or you're already planning a renovation within the next year or two, in which case an 18-inch portable is $600 spent on a machine you'll likely sell or give away. Buy the portable when the constraint is real and near-term — a lease, a landlord, a kitchen you don't own yet — not as a permanent substitute for the appliance you actually want.

FAQ

Common questions about 18-inch portables

Is an 18-inch portable dishwasher heavy to move?

It's meant to roll, not be carried. Expect to push it across a level floor without much trouble and to need a second person for any threshold, step or stair. Castors handle tile, vinyl and sealed hardwood well and struggle on thick rugs or uneven old flooring — check your kitchen floor before assuming daily moves will be effortless.

Do all 18-inch portable dishwashers need a faucet connection?

Yes, in our data every 18-inch machine is faucet-only; there's no tank version of this class. Your faucet needs a removable aerator and standard threads — pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets generally will not accept the adapter. If your kitchen tap can't take an adapter, a tank-equipped countertop machine is the only version of a portable dishwasher that will work for you.

How much bigger is 8 place settings than a countertop machine's 4 to 6?

A place setting is roughly ten items — a dinner plate, a side plate or bowl, a glass, a cup and saucer, and one person's cutlery. Eight settings works out to around eighty pieces, close to what a standard built-in dishwasher holds, versus roughly forty to seventy pieces for a four-to-six-setting countertop machine. These are manufacturer figures based on standardized crockery, so treat them as an upper bound rather than a guarantee.

Should I buy a portable now or wait and install a built-in?

Buy the portable if your constraint is real and near-term — you're renting, waiting on a lease, or don't yet have permission to modify the kitchen. Wait and install a built-in if you own the space, are annoyed by the twice-daily connect routine, or are already planning a renovation soon: a comparable built-in typically costs less than the priciest machine in this guide and never needs to be rolled anywhere.
