Buying Guide · Portable & Countertop Dishwashers

How to choose a portable dishwasher without guessing.

Three classes, a capacity number that's more marketing than measurement, and a faucet that either takes an adapter or doesn't. Get those four things right and the rest of the shopping is easy. Get one wrong and the box goes back.

By Ilane Tall · Updated August 23, 2026 · Research-based.

Every "portable dishwasher" on Amazon is sold with the same four claims: it fits anywhere, it holds more than it looks like it should, it's quiet, and it's cheap to run. One of those is usually true, one is usually exaggerated, and the other two depend entirely on your kitchen. This guide walks through the decisions in the order they actually matter — class, then capacity, then hookup, then noise and drying, then running cost — and ends with a four-question decision tree that points at a machine rather than a vague category.

Step one

The three classes, and who each is built for

"Portable dishwasher" is a category label stretched over three genuinely different appliances. Sort by what a listing actually is before you compare a single spec, because a countertop tank machine and an 18-inch machine on wheels solve different problems and cost different money.

Class one: countertop, faucet-fed

A sealed tub 16 to 22 inches wide that sits on the worktop and connects to a kitchen faucet with an adapter hose, drawing water on demand rather than from a reservoir. The COMFEE' Countertop Dishwasher, 6 Place Settings is typical of this class at 19.7 in deep by 21.6 in wide by 17.2 in tall, and it carries an ENERGY STAR certification — worth noting in a category where plenty of listings carry none. Faucet-fed machines never need refilling mid-cycle, which is their whole appeal, but they are useless the moment your tap won't accept the adapter.

Class two: countertop, tank-fed (or both)

The same size class, but built around a small reservoir — usually five litres — that you fill by hand from a jug, so the machine needs nothing but a power outlet. The EUHOMY Countertop Dishwasher Portable runs this way, rated at 40 dB with six programmes and no hookup required, and the COMFEE' Portable Dishwasher with 5L Built-in Water Tank pairs the same tank with a 360° dual spray arm and a 192°F high-temperature cycle aimed at RVs as much as apartments. Several tank machines, including that COMFEE' model, also accept a faucet hose, so you get the reservoir as a fallback rather than the only option — that combination is worth a small premium over a tank-only machine.

Class three: 18-inch freestanding, on wheels

A genuine full-height dishwasher on castors that rolls to the sink, connects through a single faucet coupler carrying supply in and waste out, and rolls back against the wall when the cycle ends. The BLACK+DECKER 18-Inch Portable Dishwasher, model BPD8B, is the reference machine in this class: 18 inches wide, ENERGY STAR certified, with a finished top that gives back the counter space the machine's footprint takes from the floor. No 18-inch machine in our database has a water tank — they are faucet-only without exception, which decides the class instantly for anyone whose tap can't take an adapter.

Between classes two and three sits a size gap worth naming: the AIRMSEN Portable Countertop Dishwasher and the HAMILTON BEACH Portable Dishwasher both sit at the upper end of the countertop price range, priced closer to the entry-level 18-inch machines than to the cheap tank models — a reminder that price alone won't tell you which class you're looking at. Read the water-source line and the external dimensions first.

Step two

Place settings, and the marketing inflation in that number

A place setting is a defined unit, not a round number a copywriter picked: one dinner plate, one side plate or bowl, one glass, one cup and saucer, and one person's cutlery — roughly ten items per setting. It's how full-size dishwashers have been rated for decades, which is why the figure is useful for comparing brands, but only if you read it the way it's meant to be read.

The COMFEE' 6 Place Settings model claims six — around 70 pieces by the brand's own count — which is roughly half a full-size load and the point at which most two- or three-person households stop planning meals around the machine. Four settings, the figure many tank-fed countertop machines claim, is one family dinner with no room for the pan. Below that, some listings quote "30+ dishes" instead of a settings count, which usually means the honest figure would look worse next to a four-place competitor.

Two things to check beyond the headline number. First, plate diameter: a machine rated for a given place-setting count often caps its rack at 10 to 10.5 inches, and plenty of American dinner plates run 11 or 12 inches — a plate that won't lie flat under the spray arm doesn't get clean, whatever the settings figure claims. Second, treat the count itself as a manufacturer claim produced with standardized crockery, not your mismatched bowls and stacking mugs; real-world capacity tends to land a little under the number on the box.

Step three

Hookup type: faucet, tank, or both

This single specification eliminates more options than price or capacity ever will, because it isn't a preference — it's a compatibility check against a physical object in your kitchen.

Faucet connection needs a tap with a removable aerator and standard threads underneath, which most straight kitchen faucets have. Pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets — now common in American kitchens and close to standard in recent rentals — generally can't take the adapter, because the spray head detaches from a hose rather than unscrewing from a fixed spout. Concealed-aerator designer taps are usually a dead end too. If that describes your kitchen, every faucet-only machine is off the table regardless of how good the rest of its spec sheet reads — that rules out the whole 18-inch class and any countertop model without a tank, including the COMFEE' 6 Place Settings machine described above.

Tank-fed machines sidestep the problem entirely: fill the reservoir from a jug, plug in, run it. The EUHOMY and the COMFEE' 5L models both work this way, and neither cares what kind of faucet, or whether there's a faucet nearby at all — which is why tank machines are the default recommendation for RVs, and for any kitchen where the sink is more than a few feet from an outlet or the counter has no room for a hose. The cost of that freedom is the reservoir itself: five liters is roughly one cycle's water, so you're refilling by hand before every wash, and a small tank is part of why tank-only machines tend to sit at four settings rather than six.

The best of both worlds is a machine that supports either connection — several countertop models, including the COMFEE' 5L tank machine referenced above, accept a faucet hose as an alternative to the reservoir. That combination is worth paying a little more for: tap at home, tank on the road, with no compromise either way. Before buying anything faucet-dependent, measure your own tap; our faucet-adapter-vs-tank comparison goes through the thread types in more detail.

Step four

Noise, drying method and cycle length

These three specs decide how much you notice the machine while it's running and how the dishes look when it's done — and none of them get much attention on a typical listing page.

Noise

The EUHOMY Countertop Dishwasher Portable is rated at 40 dB, near the quiet end of what the category advertises; most countertop machines that publish a figure at all sit closer to 50 to 52 dB. Every 10 dB is roughly a doubling of perceived loudness, so the gap between a 40 dB and a 52 dB machine is significant in a studio or open-plan kitchen. Treat any manufacturer decibel figure as a claim rather than a measurement — it's produced in undisclosed test conditions and isn't independently audited — but the relative ranking between models that publish one is still a useful signal. Where a listing gives no decibel figure at all, assume it's closer to the noisier end of the range; brands generally publicize a genuinely quiet rating when they have one.

Drying

Almost none of these machines carry a heating element dedicated to drying. Instead they rely on residual heat from the wash cycle, a circulating fan, or a door that pops open automatically at the end to vent steam. Expect some moisture left on plastics regardless of which method a given machine uses, and lean on rinse aid — it does more work in a machine without a heating element than in a full-size built-in, because it's the difference between water beading off and water sitting in a film.

Cycle length

Cycle counts and names vary by brand — Normal, Eco, Glass, Baby Care and a fast or rapid program are the common set across this category, per the manufacturers' own program lists. A high-temperature or sanitizing program, like the COMFEE' 5L tank model's 192°F function, runs longer than a quick or glass cycle and is worth choosing deliberately rather than defaulting to for every load, since it also uses more of a tank machine's limited reservoir.

Step five

Energy Star and running costs

ENERGY STAR certification means a machine has been independently verified against a federal efficiency standard for water and electricity use per cycle — it's the one credential in this category that isn't a manufacturer's own marketing claim. Two products in this guide carry it: the COMFEE' Countertop Dishwasher, 6 Place Settings and the BLACK+DECKER 18-Inch Portable Dishwasher. Plenty of listings in the category carry no certification at all, which doesn't necessarily mean they're inefficient, only that the claim hasn't been independently verified — treat an ENERGY STAR badge as real evidence and its absence as no evidence either way.

Running cost itself comes down to three inputs that vary by household: your local electricity rate per kilowatt-hour, your water and sewer rate, and how many cycles you actually run per week. A portable dishwasher's per-cycle draw is small next to a full-size built-in simply because the tub is smaller, but a household that runs two half-full loads a day spends more over a month than one that waits for a full four- or six-setting load. We work through the actual math, with assumptions stated inline, in what a portable dishwasher really costs to run — treat any flat "$X per year" figure you see elsewhere as an estimate built on assumptions you haven't seen.

Decision tree

Four questions to your machine

Answer these in order. Each question narrows the field; by the fourth you're choosing between two or three real candidates, not twenty five.

1. Can your faucet take an adapter?

Check for a removable aerator on a straight spout. If your tap is a pull-down or pull-out sprayer, or has a concealed aerator, skip straight to a tank-fed machine — the EUHOMY Countertop Dishwasher Portable or the COMFEE' 5L Built-in Water Tank model. If yes, every class in this guide, including the 18-inch machines, stays on the table.

2. Do you have floor space, or only counter space?

An 18-inch freestanding machine like the BLACK+DECKER BPD8B needs roughly 18 inches of clear floor plus door swing plus a path to the sink — but it gives that surface back as usable counter on top. If your kitchen has no spare floor, or your wall cabinets sit at the standard 18-inch clearance above the counter, stay in the countertop class and check the height line on the listing before ordering.

3. How many settings does one evening's dishes actually need?

Count plates, bowls, glasses and cutlery for a typical dinner, not a holiday. A household of two or three usually needs the six-setting range the COMFEE' 6 Place Settings model claims; a single dinner for one or two often fits inside a smaller tank machine's four-setting rack. Buying more capacity than you use just adds worktop or floor footprint for nothing.

4. Does noise or ENERGY STAR certification decide it for you?

If the machine will run in earshot of a living space you use daily, weight the 40 dB EUHOMY rating over an unrated competitor. If a lower utility bill and a verified efficiency standard matter more, favor the certified machines — the COMFEE' 6 Place Settings countertop or the BLACK+DECKER 18-Inch — over an equivalent uncertified listing.

Most buyers land on one of three answers by the fourth question: a tank-fed countertop machine because the faucet won't cooperate, a six-place faucet-fed countertop machine because it's the largest thing that still fits on a worktop, or an 18-inch machine on wheels because floor space and settings both matter more than counter space. FAQ

Common questions

What's the real difference between a 4-place and a 6-place portable dishwasher?

A place setting is a defined unit — a dinner plate, a side plate or bowl, a glass, a cup and saucer, and one person's cutlery, roughly ten items. Four settings covers one family dinner with no room for the pan; six, the figure the COMFEE' 6 Place Settings model claims at around 70 pieces, is closer to half a full-size load and the point where most two- or three-person households stop planning around the machine. Both figures are manufacturer claims measured with standardized crockery, so real capacity tends to land a little under the number.

Do I need a faucet hookup, or can I use a tank model anywhere?

A tank-fed machine like the EUHOMY Countertop Dishwasher Portable or the COMFEE' 5L Built-in Water Tank model needs only a power outlet — fill the reservoir from a jug and run it. That makes tank machines the right choice for RVs, kitchens with a sprayer faucet that won't take an adapter, or any counter too far from a usable tap. The trade-off is refilling by hand before every wash, since five liters covers roughly one cycle.

Is an 18-inch portable dishwasher worth the extra cost over a countertop model?

It depends on what you're short of. The BLACK+DECKER 18-Inch Portable Dishwasher costs more than double most countertop machines in this guide, but it's a genuine full-height dishwasher on wheels with a usable countertop surface on top — a real trade if your kitchen is short on counter space rather than floor space. If your kitchen has neither spare floor nor a compatible faucet, an 18-inch machine isn't an option at any price.

Does ENERGY STAR certification actually matter for running costs?

It's the one efficiency claim in this category that's independently verified rather than self-reported by the manufacturer. The COMFEE' 6 Place Settings countertop model and the BLACK+DECKER 18-Inch Portable Dishwasher both carry it; many competing listings carry no certification at all. An ENERGY STAR badge is real evidence of lower per-cycle water and electricity use — its absence isn't proof a machine is inefficient, only that the claim hasn't been verified.
