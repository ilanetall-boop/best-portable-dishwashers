Buying Guide · Narrow & Shallow Countertop Dishwashers

Tight counter? Depth is what actually decides it.

Countertop dishwasher listings are written to sell the machine, not to help you measure your kitchen. Manufacturers lead with width, because width is the number that looks small and unthreatening in a product title, and with place settings, because that number sells the appliance. Depth — how far the machine sticks out from the wall toward the front edge of your counter — gets buried in a spec table, folded into shipping dimensions, or left out of the listing entirely. On a standard 24 to 25-inch counter that's a real problem: a dishwasher that's too deep doesn't just look wrong, it overhangs the front edge, catches elbows, and turns every load into a two-handed lift so it doesn't tip.

This guide is built around four countertop dishwashers from our database, chosen because between them they cover the range of what "narrow and shallow" can mean in this category — from a bare-bones tank unit with no published depth at all, to the one machine here whose exterior dimensions are fully confirmed. We say exactly which is which throughout, because guessing a depth figure to make a tidy ranking would be worse than not ranking at all.

The measurement that matters

Why depth, not width, is the measurement that trips people up

Across the countertop dishwasher category broadly, exterior depth runs from just over 14 inches on the smallest tank units to nearly 20 inches on the largest six-place machines — a six-inch spread that matters enormously on a counter that's only 24 to 25 inches deep to begin with. Width, by contrast, tends to cluster in a much narrower band, because a machine has to be wide enough to hold a stack of plates side by side regardless of how compact the maker wants it to look. That's the trap: two machines with nearly identical widths on the box can differ by several inches in how far they reach toward you.

Depth decides three things width doesn't touch. First, whether the machine sits flush against the backsplash or hangs over the counter's front edge — an overhang isn't just unsightly, it's a machine you can catch a hip on and a door that, when it swings open, ends up in the walkway rather than over the counter. Second, whether it clears the edge of an under-cabinet microwave, a spice rack, or anything else mounted above a rental counter that wasn't designed with a dishwasher in mind. Third, whether the drain and inlet hoses at the back have anywhere to go without kinking against a wall.

And yet, of the four machines in this guide, only one — the COMFEE' 6-place model below — carries a full exterior depth figure in our database. The other three are sold primarily on width, tank size, and place-setting count, with depth left for the shipping-dimensions field Amazon shows lower on the page. That's not a flaw specific to these four listings; it's how this entire product category is marketed, and it's the single best argument for measuring your own counter before you click buy, which we walk through at the end of this guide.

Front clearance

Door swing: the clearance you need in front, not just on top

Most buyers measure the height gap under a wall cabinet and the depth of the counter, then stop. That covers whether the box fits where it's going to sit — it says nothing about whether you can actually open it once it's there. Countertop dishwashers open one of two ways: a door that drops forward and down, hinged at the bottom, or a lid that lifts up and back. Either way, the moment of opening needs space this page's static dimensions don't capture, because a listed depth is measured with the door closed.

A front-hinged door on a machine 19 to 20 inches deep can add another 18 to 20 inches of clearance in front of the counter when it's fully open — enough to require standing back from the counter to load it, and enough to collide with a kitchen island, a bar stool, or another person working at the counter beside you. On a narrow galley kitchen, that swing zone is often the only walking space in the room, so a machine that fits the counter's depth perfectly can still make the kitchen unusable for the two minutes it takes to load and unload it.

None of the four listings in this guide states the clearance a fully open door requires — that figure is even less commonly published than exterior depth, and we're not going to estimate one. Before you buy, look at the product photos for the hinge type (bottom-hinged door versus top-hinged lid) and, if the listing includes an installation diagram, check whether it specifies clearance for the open position. If it doesn't, assume the open door needs roughly as much space in front as the machine's depth measurement — a rough rule that at least keeps you from being surprised.

The other side of the trade

What a deeper tub buys you in capacity

Depth isn't purely a liability — it's usually where the extra wash capacity comes from. Of the four machines here, the COMFEE' 6-place model is both the deepest at a confirmed 19.7 inches and the only one with a stated place-setting count, at six. That's not a coincidence: a countertop dishwasher's interior volume grows mostly by extending front-to-back, because width is constrained by rack design and height is constrained by the wall cabinet above it. A machine that stays shallow to fit a tight counter is very often a machine that also stays small inside, holding fewer place settings or none stated at all.

Six place settings is a meaningful threshold in this category — it's roughly the point where one evening's dishes for two or three people fit into a single cycle rather than forcing you to run the machine twice or hand-wash the overflow. None of the other three machines in this guide states a place-setting figure, which on its own is a data point: sellers who have a strong number tend to print it in the title, the way COMFEE' did with "6 Place Settings." When a listing is silent on capacity, it's reasonable to assume it's smaller, not just unlisted.

The honest version of this trade-off is simple: if your counter genuinely cannot take anything over about 16 to 17 inches of depth, you are very likely choosing between a smaller wash capacity and a machine that doesn't fit at all — there's no version of this category that gives you both a shallow footprint and a full six-place interior. Anyone weighing that trade against a full sink of pots rather than a few place settings should also read can a portable dishwasher actually clean pots and pans, since capacity and cleaning power for oversized items don't move together.

Before you order

Measuring your own counter before you order

Given how inconsistently this category publishes depth, the tape measure does more work here than the spec sheet. Five measurements, taken before you add anything to cart:

  • Counter depth, front to back. Measure at the spot you intend to use, not at the widest part of the kitchen — counters step in and out around sinks and corners more often than people expect.
  • Height from counter to the underside of any cabinet above it. Standard US wall cabinets sit around 18 inches above the counter; most countertop dishwashers run 17 to 18.2 inches tall before the door or lid opens, which leaves almost no margin.
  • Clear floor space in front of the counter, out to at least the machine's stated depth, for the door swing covered above.
  • Distance to the nearest usable faucet, if you're considering a faucet-connect model rather than one of the tank-fed machines in this guide — the drain hose has a fixed length and can't be stretched.
  • The width of your intended spot, leaving an inch or two of slack on each side — a machine that's an exact width match can still be awkward to slide in and out for cleaning behind it.

Once you have those five numbers, go back to the specific listing you're considering and check its product-information table and, if depth isn't there, the shipping-dimensions box further down the page — sellers often include the true packed dimensions even when the marketing bullets don't mention depth at all. Cross-check against your own numbers before you order, not after the box arrives. If your counter turns out to be too shallow for any countertop model, the full-size class on castors is worth a look instead; the trade-offs between the two are covered in a separate comparison on this site, and the delay-start and tank-versus-faucet questions above both come up again in that context.

FAQ

Common questions about narrow and shallow dishwashers

Is depth or width the bigger risk when I order a countertop dishwasher for a small kitchen?

Depth, in almost every case. Manufacturers publish width and place-setting count prominently because those numbers sell the machine, while depth — how far it reaches from the wall toward the counter's front edge — is often buried in a shipping-dimensions field or left out of the listing entirely. Two machines with near-identical widths can differ by several inches in depth, and depth is what decides whether the machine sits flush or overhangs the counter's edge.

How much clearance do I need in front of a countertop dishwasher, not just above it?

Plan for roughly as much clearance in front as the machine is deep, to allow the door or lid to open fully. A bottom-hinged door on a 19 to 20-inch-deep machine can need another 18 to 20 inches of open floor space in front of the counter, which matters as much in a galley kitchen as the height gap under the wall cabinets does.

Does a shallower countertop dishwasher hold fewer dishes?

Generally yes. Interior volume in this category grows mostly by extending the tub front-to-back, since width and height are constrained by the rack design and the cabinet above. In this guide, the one machine with a stated place-setting count — six — is also the one with the deepest confirmed exterior dimension. A shallower machine is very often a smaller-capacity machine as well, even when its listing doesn't state a place-setting figure at all.

How do I measure my counter before ordering a narrow dishwasher?

Measure counter depth front-to-back at the exact spot you'll use, the height gap to any cabinet above it, the clear floor space in front for the door to swing open, the distance to the nearest usable faucet if you're not using a tank model, and the width of the spot with an inch or two of slack. Then check those numbers against the specific listing's product-information and shipping-dimensions fields, since depth is the figure most often missing from the marketing bullets.
