Before you buy

Four things that decide whether it works in your kitchen

01

Where the water comes from

Tank models fill from a jug — they work anywhere, including a kitchen with no usable tap, but a 5-litre tank limits the cycle. Faucet models clip to the tap with an adapter and never need refilling. Several machines here do both.

02

Place settings are optimistic

A "place setting" is a plate, bowl, glass, cup, saucer and cutlery. Four settings is one meal for a family; six is roughly half a full-size load. Check the maximum plate diameter too — most cap out around 10 to 12 inches.

03

Height, not width

Countertop machines are 17 to 18 inches tall and most need the lid or door to open upward. Standard US wall cabinets sit 18 inches above the counter. Measure that gap before anything else — it disqualifies more kitchens than price does.

04

How it dries

Almost none of these have a heated drying element. They use residual heat, a fan, or a door that pops open at the end. Expect some water left on plastics, and pick a machine with an air-dry or auto-open cycle if damp dishes will annoy you.

Why there are no star ratings on this page

Most roundups decorate every product with a rating. We won't, because we can't verify one: Amazon's product API no longer returns rating or review-count data for these machines, and inventing a number — or copying one that may be months stale — would make this page look more authoritative than it deserves to be.

So this guide is built on what is verifiable: published specifications, what's physically in the box, water source, stated capacity, dimensions and live price. Where we say something is a trade-off, it comes from the spec sheet, not from a score we made up.

FAQ

Common questions

Do portable dishwashers actually clean as well as built-in ones?

For everyday dishes, yes — they use the same mechanism: hot water sprayed under pressure with detergent, which is more thorough and hotter than hands can manage. Where they fall behind is capacity and heavy soil: a small tub means fewer dishes per cycle and less room for the spray to reach everything, so overloading is the main cause of disappointing results. Baked-on pans still need a rinse or a soak first, exactly as with a full-size machine.

Do I need a special faucet?

You need a faucet with a removable aerator and standard threads, which most straight kitchen taps have. Pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets usually don't work with the supplied adapter, and neither do some designer taps. If that describes your kitchen, buy a model with a built-in water tank — it sidesteps the problem entirely and is the reason tank models exist.

Where does the dirty water go?

Out through a drain hose that you hook over the edge of the sink; the machine pumps waste water out at the end of the cycle. Countertop machines need to sit close enough to the sink for the hose to reach and roughly level with or above the drain point. This is the second-most-common reason a machine doesn't fit a kitchen, after cabinet height.

Can I use normal dishwasher pods and rinse aid?

Pods often don't dissolve fully in the short, low-volume cycles these machines run, so most manufacturers specify powder or gel and a much smaller dose than a full-size machine. Rinse aid helps a lot here, because these dishwashers dry with residual heat and air rather than a heating element. Check your model's manual for the exact dose — overdosing a small tub leaves a film on everything.
