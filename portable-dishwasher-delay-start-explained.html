Delay start. Worth having, or just a clock?

A handful of portable dishwashers let you queue a wash for off-peak electricity hours or a quieter time of day. The feature sounds like a small luxury on a spec sheet. What it actually changes — and what a five-dollar timer plug already does for less — is a shorter list than the marketing copy suggests.

Delay start is a small line item on a countertop dishwasher's spec sheet, usually buried after the cycle count and the tank size, and it gets treated by a lot of listings as an automatic selling point. Press a button, tell the machine how many hours to wait, walk away, and the wash begins on its own later — after you've gone to bed, after you've left for work, or after the utility's cheaper electricity hours have started. That's the whole mechanism. It is a countdown timer wired to the start relay, nothing more.

What it's worth depends entirely on what you're hoping it will do. As a way to shift a wash into a quieter part of the night, it does exactly what it says. As a way to cut your electricity bill, the math below shows why the saving on a machine this small is close to invisible. And on three of the four machines in our current lineup, the published spec sheet doesn't mention delay start at all — so before anything else, this page tells you which one actually has it, and what a plain outlet timer does instead for machines that don't.

How it works

What delay start actually does, mechanically

A countertop dishwasher's control board is a small computer sitting between the buttons on the front panel and the parts that do the work — the fill valve or pump, the heating element, the wash pump and the drain pump. Pressing Start normally closes that loop immediately: the board begins running its stored sequence of fill, heat, wash, drain and dry right away.

Delay start inserts a countdown ahead of that sequence. You select a number of hours on the panel — the increments are usually whole hours, not minutes — and the control board simply waits, doing nothing, until the countdown reaches zero. Then it runs the exact cycle you selected, at the exact settings you chose, as if you had pressed Start at that later moment yourself. It is not a smarter cycle and it doesn't run any differently once it begins; it is a wait state bolted onto the front of an ordinary wash.

Two practical consequences follow from that simple description. First, you load the machine, add detergent and select the wash programme before you leave — the delay only postpones the start, so the detergent compartment sits closed and dry for the whole waiting period, which is normal and by design. Second, once the countdown finishes, the machine is unattended for the full length of the cycle, which is the point of the feature and also the thing to plan around; see the section below on running a cycle overnight versus running it while you're out.

The money question

Off-peak electricity, and why the saving is usually small

The pitch behind delay start's off-peak angle is straightforward: some utilities charge less for electricity used late at night or in the early morning, under a time-of-use rate plan, so shifting a wash into that window should shave something off the bill. It does — the question is how much, and a countertop dishwasher is a small enough appliance that the honest answer is "not much."

Work through the arithmetic with round, stated assumptions rather than a number we can't verify for any specific machine: assume a wash cycle draws on the order of 1 kWh, a reasonable ballpark for a compact, cold-fill countertop machine — actual draw varies by model and cycle, and none of the four listings above states a per-cycle kWh figure, so treat this as illustrative, not a spec. Assume a time-of-use utility charges $0.30/kWh at peak and $0.20/kWh off-peak, a $0.10 spread on the generous end of what residential plans typically publish. One cycle shifted off-peak then saves roughly ten cents; run the dishwasher daily for a month and the saving totals around three dollars.

Three dollars a month is real money over a year, but it's a rounding error against the price difference between models, and it assumes your utility offers time-of-use billing at all — most U.S. residential customers are still on a flat rate, where shifting the wash time changes nothing. If cutting electricity cost is the actual goal, the bigger levers are elsewhere: a full load instead of a half-empty one, air-drying instead of a heated dry cycle, and buying an ENERGY STAR-certified model — a spec both the SUNVIVIPRO and the Midea here carry. Delay start's off-peak case is real but small; its better case, covered next, is about noise and timing rather than money.

Overnight vs. out

A quiet cycle overnight versus running it while you're out

The stronger, more defensible use of delay start has nothing to do with electricity rates — it's about when the noise and the wait happen relative to your life. A countertop dishwasher's wash-and-dry cycle commonly runs upward of an hour, sometimes closer to two on a heavy or sanitizing programme, and for that whole time it is running a pump, cycling a heating element and occasionally sounding a drain cycle loud enough to notice in an open-plan apartment or a studio. Delay start lets you push that noise into a stretch of time when nobody's listening, rather than sitting through it or working around it.

Two versions of "nobody's listening" are not equivalent. Running the delayed cycle overnight while you're asleep in the same home means the machine is unattended but the space is occupied — if a hose slips or the machine trips a breaker, you're present to notice, even half-awake. Running it while you're out for hours is unattended in a different sense: nobody notices anything until you return. That's a meaningfully longer unattended window, and it applies to any water-using appliance, which is why manufacturer guidance for washing machines and dishwashers alike generally leans toward attended use — worth reading your specific model's manual on the point.

For countertop tank-fed machines, the plumbing involved is closer to a kettle with a drain hose than to a fixed appliance — no permanent supply-line connection, which removes one failure mode but not others (a drain hose knocked loose, a tank overflowed, an uneven counter). None of that argues against delay start; it argues for thinking about where the machine sits and how the drain hose is secured, regardless of whether the cycle runs at 2pm or 2am. The cheaper alternative

When a manual timer plug does the same job for less

Here's the honest limit of delay start as a selling point: for three of our four machines, and for most countertop dishwashers on the market generally, a mechanical or smart outlet timer plugged in line with the power cord gets you most of the same outcome for a few dollars, and it works on a machine that has no delay function of its own.

The idea is simple. Load the dishwasher, add detergent, select your wash programme — but don't press Start. Instead, plug the machine's power cord into an outlet timer set to cut power now and restore it at whatever hour you want the wash to begin. When power returns, some control panels resume the exact state they were in and will start automatically once power is live; others require a physical button press to actually begin the cycle even after power returns, which a timer alone can't do. That's the one thing to check on your specific model before you rely on a timer to finish the job unattended — read your dishwasher's manual for how it behaves after a power interruption, rather than assuming it works like a lamp.

Where a timer plug wins: it costs a fraction of the price gap between a dishwasher with delay start and one without, it works on every machine in our lineup that lacks the built-in feature, and a smart-plug version adds scheduling flexibility a fixed 12-hour countdown doesn't offer — a different start time each night, controlled from your phone. Where the built-in feature still wins: it's simpler, has no separate part to lose, and is validated by the manufacturer to behave predictably with that exact control board, which a generic timer from an unrelated brand is not.

The honest framing: buy a machine with delay start if that spec happens to come with a model you'd choose anyway for its capacity, tank or price — as it does with the SUNVIVIPRO here. Don't pay a premium for the feature alone, and don't rule out the other three on the assumption a pricier model needs a delay timer to be worth owning; a cheap outlet timer closes that gap.

FAQ

Common questions about delay start

What does delay start actually do on a portable dishwasher?

It inserts a countdown timer ahead of the wash cycle you've already selected. You load the machine, add detergent and choose your programme, then set the number of hours to wait; the control board holds the machine in a paused state until the countdown reaches zero, then runs the exact cycle you picked. It doesn't change how the cycle itself washes or dries.

Which of these four portable dishwashers has delay start?

Only the SUNVIVIPRO 18" Portable Countertop Dishwasher states a delay-start spec in its own listing — a 12-hour window. The Midea, the Hermitlux and the Hamilton Beach models we track do not mention the feature in their published specifications, so we don't assert they have it; check each listing directly if it's a deciding factor.

Does running a dishwasher on off-peak electricity actually save money?

A little, and only if your utility bills on a time-of-use plan. Assuming a cycle draws roughly 1 kWh and a $0.10 spread between peak and off-peak rates — both estimates, since none of these listings states a per-cycle kWh figure — one shifted cycle saves around ten cents, or roughly three dollars a month run daily. Full-size appliances and time-of-use plans with a wider rate spread produce a bigger saving than a small countertop dishwasher does.

Can I use a smart plug or timer instead of buying a dishwasher with delay start?

Often, yes. Load the machine and select the programme without pressing Start, then let an outlet timer cut and later restore power at the time you want the wash to begin. The catch is that some control panels require a manual button press to begin the cycle even after power returns, rather than resuming automatically — check your specific model's manual or listing Q&A before relying on a timer to finish the job while you're away.
