How-To · Living With an 18-Inch Portable

Moving and storing a portable dishwasher between uses.

An 18-inch dishwasher on castors is not a countertop appliance — it is a genuine dishwasher that happens to roll. Some owners wheel it to the sink and back without a second thought; others try it twice and leave it parked permanently. Here is what the wheels, the weight, and the hose routine actually involve before you decide which owner you'll be.

"Portable" on a dishwasher listing covers two entirely different arrangements, and the confusion causes most of the buyer's remorse in this category. A countertop machine is portable the way a microwave is: pick it up, put it somewhere else. An 18-inch freestanding dishwasher on castors is portable the way a rolling suitcase is — it moves on its own wheels, across a flat floor, one room at most, and it is emphatically not something you lift. This guide is only about that second kind: the full-height machine that lives near the kitchen and rolls to the sink to run a cycle.

That distinction matters because the daily reality is different from what the word "portable" implies. You are not carrying an appliance. You are pushing roughly 70 to 100 pounds of steel and water across a floor, connecting two lines to a faucet, running a cycle that can last close to two hours, then disconnecting and pushing it back. Whether that is a two-minute chore or an ongoing argument with your kitchen depends on specifics most listings never mention: your floor surface, the distance to your sink, and whether you are the one doing the pushing.

Two classes

Which machines are actually meant to move

Only one class of dishwasher in this category is designed to be relocated on a routine, load-by-load basis: the 18-inch freestanding model on castors, such as the HAMILTON BEACH Portable Dishwasher with Wheels, the SD-9263SSC 18-inch stainless model, the HAMILTON BEACH Portable Dishwasher in stainless/platinum, and the BLACK+DECKER BPD8B 18-Inch. Every one of these ships with a full set of locking or swivel castors under the base, a finished countertop surface on top designed to be seen and used while parked, and a single quick-connect coupler that handles both water supply and drain through the same faucet connection. The whole machine is engineered around the assumption that it lives somewhere other than at the sink and travels there to run.

A countertop dishwasher is not built for this. It sits permanently on a worktop, and while nothing stops you from unplugging one and sliding it to a different spot, that is a repositioning, not a routine — most countertop models have no wheels at all, a plastic or light-steel shell meant to stay put, and an inlet hose short enough that "moving it" usually means moving it out of the kitchen entirely. If your plan is to store a dishwasher in a closet and bring it to the sink for every load, an 18-inch model on castors is the only class actually designed for that job; trying to do it with a countertop unit means carrying, not rolling, and carrying a water-filled appliance is a different and worse problem, covered below.

Within the 18-inch class itself, the four machines above are close to interchangeable on this specific question — none of the size or spec data we have for them describes anything about the castor hardware, wheel diameter, or locking mechanism, so there is nothing here to differentiate one from another on rollability. Check the individual listing photos for wheel type before you buy if smooth-floor glide or carpet-edge clearance is a priority; it is not a spec any of these listings states in words.

Weight

What you are actually pushing

None of the four machines in this comparison lists a shipping or unit weight in the specification data we have, which is itself worth noting: weight is one of the specs manufacturers in this category are inconsistent about publishing, and it is exactly the number that determines whether daily rolling is comfortable or a chore. As a general fact about the appliance class rather than a claim about any specific model here, 18-inch portable dishwashers with a stainless tub and full castor base typically fall in the neighborhood of 70 to 100 pounds empty — heavier than almost anything else in a kitchen that moves on wheels, and enough that pushing one over a raised threshold or a floor transition strip takes real effort, not a light touch.

Empty weight is only half the number that matters. A dishwasher never runs empty — it fills with several gallons of wash water partway through the cycle, and a gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds. A machine holding even three or four gallons mid-cycle is carrying an extra 25 to 35 pounds on top of its empty weight, all of it sloshing at floor level in a tub with a door that is not necessarily as watertight as it is when new. This is the specific reason every manufacturer instruction for this class says the same thing: never move the machine while water is inside it, and never move it mid-cycle. Rolling a machine that has just finished, before it has fully drained, risks tipping water out around the door seal and onto the floor you are rolling it across.

The practical routine that follows from this: let the cycle complete fully, including the drain phase, before disconnecting anything. Most cycles end with the pump running the tub dry: wait for it. If you are the one doing the daily rolling and you have any doubt about handling 100-plus pounds on wheels — particularly over a doorway lip or from tile onto vinyl — treat the empty-versus-mid-cycle distinction as more than a technicality; it is the difference between a machine that rolls easily and one that tips.

Every single time

The hose and cord routine, each time

An 18-inch portable connects and disconnects at the faucet for every wash, and the routine is the same each time whether you do it once a day or twice a week:

  • Roll the machine into position. It needs to sit close enough to the sink that the supply-and-drain coupler reaches the faucet without stretching or kinking, with the power cord able to reach an outlet on its own dedicated circuit — see our guide on what a portable dishwasher needs from your outlet if you have not already confirmed the machine won't share a circuit with anything else running.
  • Remove the faucet aerator if it is not already off, and attach the quick-connect coupler included with the machine. This is the same connection point used for both bringing water in and pumping wastewater back out, which is what lets these machines skip a separate drain-hose-over-the-sink-edge arrangement that countertop models rely on.
  • Plug in the power cord to a grounded outlet, load the machine, select a cycle, and turn on the faucet fully before starting — the machine draws its own water through the coupler rather than filling from a tank, so the tap has to stay on and undisturbed for the length of the cycle.
  • Run the cycle. A Normal or Heavy programme on an 18-inch portable commonly runs well over an hour; a quick or Rapid programme where the model offers one is shorter but washes less thoroughly. The machine occupies the space in front of the sink, and often blocks the sink itself, for the entire run.
  • Wait for the full drain phase at the end before touching the coupler — disconnecting early can release water still in the line.
  • Turn off the faucet, disconnect the coupler, unplug the cord, and roll the machine back to wherever it lives. Coil the cord and secure the coupler to the machine (most have a clip or holder for this) rather than letting either drag on the floor during the move.

None of this is difficult in isolation. What it adds up to is a five-to-ten-minute process bookending every wash, performed at floor level, involving a faucet connection that has to seat correctly or it leaks. For someone doing this daily, it becomes routine within a week or two. For someone who finds any of the individual steps annoying — the aerator removal, the coupler seating, the waiting for full drain — it tends to become the reason the machine gets used less often than intended, sink loads pile up, and the "portable" dishwasher slowly turns into a stationary one.

Floor and casters

Floor protection and the caster question

Castors roll well on hard, flat, continuous surfaces — sealed hardwood, vinyl, tile with minimal grout-line depth, and laminate. They roll poorly to not at all on thick carpet, deep-pile rugs, and uneven or heavily textured tile, and they stop dead at thresholds and transition strips higher than roughly a quarter inch. If your path from storage spot to sink crosses any of those, "portable" may mean "portable in theory" — check the actual floor path before assuming the machine will glide as easily as it does in a product video shot on a showroom floor.

Hard flooring that rolls well is not automatically flooring you want a heavy appliance rolling on repeatedly. Hardwood in particular can dent, scratch or lose finish under repeated caster traffic on the same path, especially if grit gets trapped under a wheel. A caster mat, a runner rug with a low, dense pile, or simply varying the path slightly each time reduces the wear pattern. None of the four machines in this comparison lists rubber-tired versus hard-plastic castors as a spec, so the manufacturer photos are the only way to check before buying — hard plastic castors are harder on wood floors than rubber-rimmed ones.

Water is the other floor consideration. A machine that leaks slightly at the coupler, or that gets rolled before it has fully drained, drips on whatever floor it crosses. On hardwood or laminate — materials that swell and warp with repeated moisture exposure — this matters more than on tile or vinyl. If your kitchen floor is wood and your storage spot is more than a few feet from the sink, a small towel kept at the parking spot and a quick wipe of the coupler before rolling are worth the ten seconds they take.

Where it lives

Where it lives when it is not at the sink

The finished top surface that BLACK+DECKER and the other 18-inch machines here are built with is not a cosmetic afterthought — it is the reason the parking spot works at all. A machine that has to be tucked into a genuinely out-of-the-way corner to look acceptable is a machine people stop rolling out; one with a usable countertop on top earns its floor space back by holding the microwave, a cutting board, or the mail, which is exactly the trade the BPD8B's own product photography leads with.

The practical requirements for a parking spot: a flat, hard floor patch roughly 18 to 19 inches wide plus clearance for the door to swing open when it eventually needs cleaning or a filter check; a path to the sink clear of furniture, pet bowls and floor mats that would otherwise need moving every time; and, ideally, a spot not directly in a walking lane, since a machine parked mid-traffic gets bumped, and bumping a machine on castors tends to roll it slightly rather than stopping it dead. Against a wall near the sink, in a kitchen island gap, or in the "dead" end of a galley kitchen are the three spots that come up most often in kitchens built around this appliance class. For a broader look at fitting any portable dishwasher — countertop or rolling — into a tight footprint, see where to put a portable dishwasher when there is no room.

One detail worth planning around: the cord and coupler need somewhere to live when the machine is parked, ideally clipped or coiled against the unit rather than left loose, both so nobody trips on them and so they are not lying on a floor that gets mopped or walked across barefoot.

FAQ

Common questions

Is it safe to move a portable dishwasher while it still has water in it?

No. Manufacturer guidance for this appliance class is consistent: never relocate the machine until the cycle, including the final drain phase, has fully completed. A machine holding even a few gallons mid-cycle carries a significant amount of extra weight sloshing at floor level, and rolling it risks water escaping around the door seal onto your floor. Wait for the pump to finish draining, then disconnect and move it.

How heavy is an 18-inch portable dishwasher?

None of the four models in this comparison publishes a shipping or unit weight in the data we have, so treat any specific number you see elsewhere as unverified until you check the current listing. As a general fact about this appliance class rather than a claim about a specific model, machines of this type — stainless tub, full castor base — commonly fall in the range of roughly 70 to 100 pounds empty, before accounting for water added mid-cycle.

Will the castors damage my kitchen floor?

Castors roll cleanly on sealed hardwood, tile, vinyl and laminate, but repeated traffic on the same path can wear a finish over time, particularly on wood, and a machine that leaks slightly at the faucet coupler can leave moisture on a floor that doesn't tolerate it well. None of the listings in this comparison specifies rubber versus hard-plastic castor material, so check the product photos, and consider a caster mat or varying the rolling path if your floor is hardwood.

Do I need to disconnect the hoses every single time I use it?

Yes, for this appliance class. These machines use a single quick-connect coupler at the faucet for both water supply and drain, and it has to be attached before each cycle and removed afterward so the machine can be rolled back to its storage spot. There is no version of this class that stays permanently plumbed in while also remaining mobile — permanent connection is what a built-in dishwasher is for.
