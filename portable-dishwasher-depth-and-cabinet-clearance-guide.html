Buying advice · Depth & cabinet clearance

The depth number the listing photo won't show you.

"Large," "compact" and "slim" are marketing words, not measurements. Two machines both sold as countertop dishwashers can differ by two and a half inches of depth — enough to decide whether a cabinet door closes behind it or a shelf edge gets in the way. Here's how to read the numbers that actually matter, including one clearance rule most buyers have never heard of.

Every portable dishwasher listing gives you a size word before it gives you a number. "Large capacity." "Slim design." "Compact for small kitchens." Those words tell a shopper almost nothing, because the category has no standard footprint — a "large" countertop machine and a "slim" one can both be roughly the same height while differing by more than two inches in how far they stick out from the wall. That gap is the one measurement most buyers never check, and it's the one that decides whether the machine fits the shelf, the cabinet door still closes, and the drain hose still reaches the sink.

This guide walks through three real listings — two that disclose an exact depth figure and one that doesn't — to show what a complete spec sheet looks like, what a thin one leaves out, and the specific clearance number for tank refilling that none of the general "measure your cabinet" advice mentions. The category

Why "countertop dishwasher" doesn't mean one standard size

A 24-inch built-in dishwasher is 24 inches wide, full stop — the category name is a real dimension. A "countertop dishwasher" or "portable dishwasher" carries no such guarantee. It's a description of where the machine sits and how it gets its water, not a size class, and three listings using nearly identical language can ship three different boxes.

You can see the spread inside a single brand. AIRMSEN sells a model simply called the Portable Countertop Dishwasher that measures 16.75 inches wide, 16.86 inches deep and 18.05 inches tall — and a second model, marketed as the Large Slim Countertop Dishwasher, that the listing describes as "only 14.2 inches deep" specifically because that's shallower than the brand's own standard footprint, while also being "wider and taller" to make up the interior volume. Same brand, same general category, two different shapes solving two different counter problems. The same pattern shows up across brands — our comparison of Danby and EdgeStar's portable dishwashers turns up similarly mismatched footprints under similar-sounding names.

The practical result: the word on the listing title tells you almost nothing about whether the box will fit your counter. Only the number does, and the number isn't always on the same part of the page.

Reading the spec sheet

Reading depth, width and height correctly

Dimensions on an appliance listing are conventionally given in the order width × depth × height — sometimes written W × D × H, sometimes just three numbers with no labels at all. Depth is the measurement that matters most for cabinet fit, because it's how far the machine projects out from the wall or backsplash, but it's also the one third-party listing copy mixes up most often, since "depth" and "width" get used loosely in casual product descriptions in a way they never would be by an engineer.

The AIRMSEN Portable Countertop Dishwasher's listing does this correctly and completely: 16.75"W x 16.86"D x 18.05"H, spelled out with the axis labeled on each number. That's the standard a shopper should look for — three numbers, each one tagged, so there's no ambiguity about which figure is depth. Compare that with a listing that gives only a size category and a single standout figure, which is exactly what the EUHOMY Countertop Dishwasher Portable does: its own copy states a recommended vertical clearance ("more than 18 inches... between the countertop and upper cabinets") but doesn't give a width-by-depth-by-height breakdown in the bullets at all, beyond calling the unit size "Large." That's not dishonest — it's a different, and less useful, way of describing a box.

When a listing gives you a size word and a clearance recommendation but no depth number, treat that absence as information. It means you need to open the product's dimensions tab or ask the seller directly before you can compare it, inch for inch, against a model like the AIRMSEN that spells every axis out. Never estimate a missing dimension from a photo — product photography is cropped and angled to make every appliance look roughly the same size on a phone screen.

One more mix-up worth watching for, as a general rule across imported small appliances: some listings quote a dimension in centimeters inside a bullet that otherwise reads in inches, usually copied from an international spec sheet without conversion. None of the three machines in this guide does that, but it's common enough in the broader category that it's worth a second look any time a number feels implausibly large or small for the stated size class.

The number that gets missed

The 23.5-inch cabinet height rule for tank refilling

Most general buying advice for this category repeats a single clearance figure: leave roughly 18 to 20 inches between the countertop and the bottom of the wall cabinets above it, because that's how tall a typical countertop dishwasher is and you need the box to physically sit in the gap. That number answers one question — does the machine fit under the cabinet at all — and it's the one our own guide to published wash temperatures and most competitor roundups quote.

It does not answer a second question: can you actually open the lid and refill the tank once the machine is sitting in that gap? That's a different, larger clearance figure, and it's easy to miss because most listings never state it. The AIRMSEN Large Slim Countertop Dishwasher's own listing does, in its first bullet, as a direct tip to the buyer: "Allow at least 23.5 inches cabinet height for water tank refilling." That's roughly five and a half inches more than the basic fit clearance — enough that a machine which slides neatly under a standard cabinet can still leave you unable to lift the lid far enough to pour a jug of water in without pulling the whole unit forward first.

The reason this gets missed is structural, not careless: the 18-to-20-inch figure describes the machine at rest, closed, doing nothing. The 23.5-inch figure describes the machine mid-task, lid open, water being poured — a dynamic clearance that depends on how far the specific lid or door swings, which varies by model and isn't implied by the closed-box height at all. A buyer who remembers "about 18 inches" from a general guide and applies it to a tank-fill model can end up with a machine that fits the shelf but has to be dragged out onto the counter every single refill.

Not every machine needs the larger number. Faucet-connected models that never need hand-refilling only need the static fit clearance, because nothing has to open upward past the closed-box height during normal use. The distinction is specifically a tank-refill problem, and it's worth checking for on any listing before you assume "it fits under my cabinets" settles the question.

Before you order

Measuring your own counter and cabinet clearance

Five measurements, taken before you order rather than after the box arrives, cover every failure mode in this guide:

1. Depth of the shelf or counter run

Measure from the front edge of the counter or shelf straight back to the wall or backsplash, at the exact spot the machine will sit. If that number is less than the model's stated depth, the machine will either overhang the front edge or need to sit further back than the water inlet and drain hoses reach.

2. Width of the run, including anything beside it

Measure side to side, and subtract a couple of inches if a cabinet door, a drawer pull, or an appliance beside the spot needs room to open without hitting the dishwasher's casing.

3. Vertical clearance to the underside of the wall cabinet, closed

Measure straight up from the counter surface to the bottom of the cabinet above. Compare this against the machine's closed height — this is the 18-to-20-inch static-fit number most general advice quotes, and it only tells you whether the box fits at rest.

4. Vertical clearance with the lid or door at full open swing

This is the number most listings skip and the one the AIRMSEN Large Slim's 23.5-inch tip is addressing. If the machine has a tank you refill by hand, measure (or ask the seller for) how far the lid needs to swing open, add that to the counter height, and compare the total against your cabinet clearance — not the closed-box height.

5. Distance to the sink, for the drain hose

Countertop machines pump waste water out through a hose hooked over the sink edge, and that hose has a fixed length. A spot that passes every depth and height check but sits too far from the sink still fails. The full mechanics of this — hose length, hook height, and what happens if the run is too long — are covered in where a portable dishwasher's drain hose actually goes.

A cardboard mock-up — a box cut to the stated width, depth and height, taped shut and set in the actual spot — remains the single most reliable way to catch a problem before the real machine arrives, especially for the lid-swing clearance in step 4, which is easy to get wrong from a tape measure alone.

When it doesn't fit

What happens when a unit is too deep for its shelf

The most common failure isn't dramatic — the machine isn't damaged and it isn't the wrong product, it's simply a few inches too deep for the spot it was meant to go. The visible symptoms are usually one of three things: the unit overhangs the counter edge and becomes a bump hazard, an adjacent cabinet door can no longer swing fully open, or the lid can't tilt back far enough to refill the tank without first pulling the whole machine forward onto the counter — exactly the scenario the 23.5-inch rule above is meant to prevent.

If that happens, don't run a load in it first. Most marketplace return policies require the item back in resellable condition, and a used dishwasher — water in the tub, detergent residue, a run cycle on the control board — is far harder to return than an unused one. Before you do anything else: measure again to confirm it's genuinely a fit problem and not a setup mistake, and photograph the machine in place against a tape measure, which is useful both for your own records and if the seller asks for evidence.

From there, repack it the way it arrived — original box, original foam or packaging inserts, manual and accessories included — and start the return through the marketplace listing rather than contacting the manufacturer first; Amazon purchases are returned through Amazon's own process, and doing it that way keeps the refund timeline predictable. Keep the box until the return is confirmed. If you've already connected the hose or filled the tank, drain and dry the unit fully before repacking, since a wet tub shipped back in a cardboard box is a problem for everyone in the chain.

The cheaper fix, and the one worth trying before any of the above: measure the actual gap you have left after the oversized unit is removed, and shop the next model by depth number first, size word last. A machine with a stated depth an inch or two shallower than the one you're returning — not a different "size category" — is the safer bet the second time.

FAQ

Common questions about depth and clearance

Does "countertop dishwasher" guarantee a standard size?

No. Unlike a 24-inch built-in, "countertop" or "portable" describes where the machine sits and how it's supplied with water, not a fixed footprint. Depth alone varies by more than two inches between real listings in this category — compare AIRMSEN's own 14.2-inch Large Slim model against its 16.86-inch Portable model. Always check the stated dimensions rather than assuming a size word implies a specific number.

What's the difference between the 18-to-20-inch rule and the 23.5-inch rule?

The 18-to-20-inch figure is the static clearance needed for a closed countertop dishwasher to physically sit under a standard wall cabinet. The 23.5-inch figure, stated directly in the AIRMSEN Large Slim's own listing, is the larger clearance needed to tilt the lid fully open and refill the water tank by hand once the machine is already in place. A machine can satisfy the first number and still fail the second if it's a tank-fill model.

How do I measure my cabinet clearance before ordering?

Measure depth from the counter's front edge straight back to the wall, measure width including room for any adjacent cabinet door, measure vertical clearance to the cabinet above with the unit closed, and — if the machine has a hand-filled tank — add the lid's open swing to the counter height for a second vertical check. Finally confirm the spot is within drain-hose reach of the sink. A cardboard mock-up cut to the listed dimensions is the most reliable way to catch a problem before the real unit ships.

What if the dishwasher arrives and it's too deep for the shelf?

Don't run a load in it first — most marketplace returns require the item unused and in resellable condition. Measure again to confirm the fit problem, photograph it in place against a tape measure, then repack it in the original box with all inserts and accessories and start the return through the marketplace listing. If the tank was already filled, drain and dry the unit completely before repacking.
