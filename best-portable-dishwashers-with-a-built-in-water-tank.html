Buying Guide · Tank-Fed Countertop Dishwashers

No faucet, no problem. Fill it from a jug.

A built-in water tank is the one spec that makes a dishwasher work in a kitchen with no usable tap at all — a sprayer faucet, a rental with a designer spout, an RV with no plumbing hookup to speak of. Every countertop dishwasher needs a water source, and there are exactly two: a hose clipped to the kitchen faucet, or a reservoir you fill by hand. The tank is the one that works when the faucet won't cooperate — pull-down sprayers, concealed aerators, a rental with no straight spout at all — and it's the only version of this appliance an RV or a dorm room can use without plumbing. This guide is narrow on purpose: not the whole portable-dishwasher category, just the tank-fed machines, what the reservoir actually does mid-cycle, and where its limits show up.

The mechanism

How a tank model works, and where the water goes afterwards

A tank-fed countertop dishwasher is built around a sealed reservoir instead of a hose fitting. Open a flap or a small door on the top or side of the machine, pour water in from a jug or a pot, close it, and the pump draws from that reservoir instead of from a live tap. No adapter, no aerator threads to match, no faucet at all — the machine only needs a standard power outlet.

What happens to that water once it's inside is the same basic mechanism every dishwasher uses, portable or built-in: the pump doesn't drain and refill the tub between every phase of the cycle. It draws water into a sump, sprays it through the arms, lets it fall back, and recirculates that same water through wash and rinse phases before drawing fresh water again for the final rinse. That recirculation is the reason a five-litre fill can do the work that would otherwise take several times as much — the reservoir is topping up a sump, not supplying a continuous flow.

Filling the tank solves supply, not drainage. Every model in this guide still finishes a cycle by pumping wastewater out through a drain hose hooked over the edge of the sink, the same way a faucet-connected machine does — a tank gets you out of needing a compatible tap, but it doesn't get you out of needing a sink nearby to drain into. Machines without a tank at all skip this question entirely by being faucet-only, which is most of what separates the two classes covered in our countertop vs 18-inch comparison.

The number that matters

Five litres: how many cycles that really is

Five litres — 1.32 US gallons, the figure printed on more than one of these listings — is the standard reservoir size in this class, and it is specified as enough water for one wash cycle, not one day's dishes and not multiple loads. Fill it, run one program, and by the time the cycle ends the tank is effectively spent; there's no reserve sitting behind it for a second run.

That figure also explains why tank machines tend to cap out at smaller capacities than their faucet-fed siblings. The NOVETE Portable Countertop Dishwasher, one of the picks below, ships with a 5-litre tank and is rated for a real four-place-setting load rather than the six settings a larger faucet-connected machine can claim — a smaller reservoir means less water budgeted for rinsing, and rinsing is what a bigger rack actually needs more of. If you routinely wash a full evening's dishes for three or four people in one sitting, plan on refilling the tank at least once, or on running a faucet-connected machine instead — a decision our loading guide can help you plan around, since overloading a small tub is the single biggest reason a tank cycle disappoints.

One more assumption worth stating: none of these listings publish an independent test of how many place settings a given fill actually clears at a stated soil level. The four- and six-setting figures on these products are manufacturer claims produced with standardized crockery, and real capacity — like the general place-setting math across this whole category — tends to land a little under the number on the box.

The trade-off

Tank fill against faucet hookup, cycle by cycle

Laid out step by step, the two water sources ask something different of you at almost every stage.

Before the cycle starts

A faucet-connected machine asks you to check that your tap has a removable aerator and standard threads, attach the supplied hose, and turn the water on — a five-minute job done once, or occasionally checked. A tank machine asks you to fill a jug or a pot at the sink, pour it into the reservoir, and repeat until the fill line is reached — a job done before every single wash, with no way to skip it.

During the cycle

Once running, a faucet-connected machine draws whatever water each phase needs and never thinks about supply again. A tank machine is working from a fixed budget: everything from the first wash spray to the last rinse comes out of the same five litres, which is why tank-fed machines tend to run shorter or more conservative cycles than their faucet-fed counterparts, and why a long or high-temperature program — the kind built for baby bottles or greasy pans — spends the reservoir faster than a quick glass cycle does.

After the cycle ends

Both classes finish the same way: a pump pushes dirty water out through a drain hose and over the sink edge. The faucet machine is done and disconnects with a twist of the coupler; the tank machine is also done, but if you're running a second load, the reservoir is empty again and the whole fill step repeats.

None of this makes one class better than the other — it makes them suited to different kitchens. A faucet hookup asks less of you cycle after cycle; a tank is the only option at all when there's no faucet a machine can physically attach to. Full-size 18-inch portables, for comparison, skip the tank question entirely: every model in our 18-inch portable dishwasher guide is faucet-only, with no reservoir fallback if the tap doesn't cooperate.

The interruption

The refill prompt, and the machines that handle it badly

A fixed five-litre budget means a long or high-temperature program can, in principle, ask for more water than the tank was filled with. Most tank machines handle this with some form of water-level sensor that either pauses the cycle and signals for a top-up, or simply won't start a program if the reservoir reads too low to finish it. The NOVETE model is explicit about this in its own listing, describing an automatic water-level indicator built into the tank — a spec worth having, because it turns a potential mid-cycle interruption into a visible prompt instead of a machine that quietly runs dry.

Not every listing in this guide states how its unit behaves when the tank runs low mid-program. Where a product's own spec sheet doesn't describe a low-water indicator or an auto-pause behavior, we're not going to assert one exists — check the current Amazon listing and the printed manual before assuming a machine will flag a low reservoir gracefully rather than stall or error out. This is also the point at which running the sanitizing or high-temperature cycle on a full evening's worth of dishes is the most likely to trip a refill prompt, simply because it's the program asking the most of the reservoir.

If your machine does interrupt mid-cycle, refilling and resuming is normally as simple as topping the tank back up to the fill line and closing the door — but a cycle that pauses partway through is also the most common single complaint about tank machines, and it's worth reading a listing's Q&A section for exactly this behavior before buying if you plan to run long or heavy loads regularly. Persistent stalls, error lights or a machine that won't restart after a refill are covered in our portable dishwasher troubleshooting guide.

Upkeep

Keeping a tank clean between uses

A sealed reservoir that sits full of standing water between washes is exactly the kind of enclosed, damp space that grows odor and mold given enough time — a problem faucet-connected machines don't have, since they never hold water when idle. A tank model's upkeep routine is short, but skipping it is the most common reason a perfectly good machine starts to smell.

  • Empty the tank after each wash rather than leaving the fill in for the next cycle "just in case." Standing water left for days is where odor starts.
  • Wipe the reservoir dry with a cloth after emptying it, especially around the fill opening and any seal or gasket, since trapped moisture in a seam is harder to air out than an open surface.
  • Leave the door or lid cracked open between uses instead of sealing the machine shut. Several of these models include an air-dry or air-circulation function for exactly this reason — use it, or approximate it by leaving the unit ajar.
  • Run an empty cycle periodically with a dishwasher-safe cleaner or a diluted vinegar rinse to clear mineral buildup and any residue the tank and pump have accumulated, particularly if your water is hard.

None of this is specific to one brand or model — it's the same general appliance-care logic that applies to a thermos or a water-cooler reservoir: empty it, dry it, don't seal it up wet. FAQ

Common questions about tank models

How often do I need to refill a 5-litre tank?

Once per cycle at minimum, since the reservoir is specified as enough water for one wash program, not multiple loads. A long or high-temperature program can ask for more water than a quick cycle and may prompt a mid-cycle top-up on machines with a low-water indicator, like the NOVETE model's automatic water-level sensor. Plan on filling the tank fresh before every single wash.

Do tank dishwashers still need to be near a sink?

Yes. A built-in tank solves the supply side of the machine — no faucet needed to fill it — but every model still drains dirty water out through a hose hooked over the edge of a sink at the end of the cycle. A tank gives you more freedom in where the machine sits, not freedom from needing a drain point nearby.

What happens if the tank runs dry mid-cycle?

It depends on the machine's water-level sensor, and not every listing in this category states how its unit behaves. Models with an automatic low-water indicator, like the NOVETE Portable Countertop Dishwasher, are built to flag the tank running low rather than run the pump dry. Where a listing doesn't describe this behavior, check the manual or the Amazon Q&A section before assuming it will handle a mid-cycle refill gracefully.

Can a tank model also connect to a faucet?

Some can. The NOVETE Portable Countertop Dishwasher ships with both a built-in tank and an inlet hose, so it can run either way. Other machines in this guide, including both COMFEE' Minilite models, are specified as tank-only with no hookup at all — check a listing's own water-source description rather than assuming every tank machine also accepts a faucet.
