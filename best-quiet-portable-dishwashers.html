Buying Guide · Noise & Decibel Ratings

Quiet enough to run while you're in the room.

In a studio, decibels matter more than programs. A machine three feet from your desk or your couch is a different purchase from one tucked behind a kitchen door, and most listings bury the one number that actually predicts how much you'll notice it. Most portable-dishwasher listings lead with programs, place settings and price, and bury the decibel figure two bullets down — if they publish one at all. That ordering makes sense for a machine hidden behind a cabinet door in a normal kitchen. It makes no sense for a studio, a one-room apartment, or an open-plan kitchen with no wall between the sink and the sofa, where the dishwasher runs in the same acoustic space as everything else you're doing that evening. In those layouts, a 12 dB gap between two machines matters more than an extra wash cycle ever will.

This guide starts with what the numbers actually mean, works through why a countertop machine can be louder than the built-in it's replacing, ranks the five machines below from quietest published rating to loudest, and ends with placement tricks and delay-start scheduling that can make a merely average machine livable in a small space.

The scale

What 40, 45 and 55 dB actually sound like

~40 dB

Roughly a quiet library, or the hum of a refrigerator from across the kitchen. The quietest published rating in this category, and the figure the two EUHOMY machines below carry.

02

~45–50 dB

A quiet home office, light rainfall on a window, or a hushed conversation from the next room. Most countertop dishwashers that decline to publish a number likely sit somewhere in this band.

03

~52–55 dB

Normal conversation at a few feet, or a window air conditioner on a low setting. The Hamilton Beach machine below is rated here — audible across a studio, not disruptive across a house.

04

Every +10 dB ≈ 2×

The decibel scale is logarithmic: each 10 dB increase is roughly a doubling of perceived loudness. The gap between a 40 dB and a 52 dB machine isn't a rounding error — it reads as noticeably more than half again as loud.

These reference points are common acoustic benchmarks, not measurements of any specific dishwasher — they exist to give the manufacturer's number somewhere to land. A rated 40 dB dishwasher won't sound silent; it will sound like a refrigerator with a fan and a faint splash of water, audible if the room is otherwise quiet and easy to talk over. A rated 52 dB machine sounds more like a low conversation happening in the kitchen — fine if you're in another room, present if you're at the desk six feet away.

Why the gap exists

Why compact machines are often louder than built-ins

A full-size built-in dishwasher is recessed into a cabinet run, sandwiched between two panels of particleboard, sitting on a floor rather than a hollow countertop, and usually wrapped in its own sound-insulation blanket around the tub — several layers of material between the pump and your ears before the sound ever reaches open air. A portable or countertop machine has almost none of that. It's a thin-walled plastic or steel shell standing directly on the worktop, in the open room, with the hard countertop surface underneath it free to act as a soundboard for any vibration the pump and motor produce.

The motor itself works harder, too. A compact machine has to move water and pressure through a much smaller pump than a built-in's, running closer to its mechanical limit for a given wash intensity, and that pump usually sits inches from the base rather than isolated in a dedicated compartment. None of this means portable machines are badly engineered — it's a direct consequence of being small, freestanding and unenclosed rather than built into cabinetry. If your kitchen can support a genuine built-in installation instead, our guide to full-size 18-inch portables on wheels covers the closest thing to that experience without plumbing work.

What follows from this: a manufacturer's decibel rating on a countertop machine is measured under conditions the brand controls and rarely discloses, so treat the number as a claim and the relative ranking between machines that publish one as the more useful signal. A listing with no decibel figure at all isn't necessarily loud — but brands that have a genuinely quiet number tend to lead with it, so its absence is worth noting rather than ignoring.

Beyond the spec sheet

Placement tricks that cut perceived noise

The decibel rating on the box is a starting point, not the final word — where and how the machine sits changes what you actually hear, sometimes by more than the gap between a 40 dB and a 52 dB rating.

  • Put something soft underneath it. A hard countertop or a hollow cabinet top acts as a soundboard, carrying vibration from the pump into the surface and amplifying it. A dense silicone mat or a folded towel under the machine's feet absorbs a meaningful share of that transmitted vibration before it ever reaches the counter.
  • Keep it off a shared wall. In a studio or open-plan layout, a few feet of distance from a bed, desk or sofa reduces both direct sound and the vibration path into furniture touching the same wall or floor.
  • Check the drain hose for kinks. A pinched or kinked drain hose makes the pump work harder at the end of the cycle, which is usually the loudest few minutes of any wash — a straight, unobstructed hose run reduces that spike. Our guide to faucet adapters versus built-in tanks covers hose routing for both connection types.
  • Never run it half-empty on purpose. Water striking bare metal or an empty rack section is noticeably louder than water striking a full load of dishes, which absorbs and dampens the spray. Waiting for a fuller load is quieter, not just more efficient.
  • Leave clearance behind the unit. A few inches of open air behind the machine, rather than pushing it flush against a wall or backsplash, gives the pump and drain line room to vent sound instead of reflecting it straight back into the room.

None of these tricks close a genuinely wide gap — a 52 dB machine on a padded mat is still louder than a 40 dB machine on bare countertop — but they can be the difference between a quiet machine you never notice and a quiet machine that occasionally interrupts a phone call.

Scheduling around noise

Night cycles and delay start

The simplest fix for a noisy dishwasher in a small space isn't a quieter machine — it's not being in the room while it runs. A delay-start function lets you load the machine after dinner and schedule the wash for a time you won't be sitting six feet away, whether that's while you're out, or overnight if the machine is far enough from where you sleep.

Of the five machines in this comparison, the HAMILTON BEACH Portable Dishwasher is the only one whose listing publishes a delay-start feature, letting you set the wash to begin later rather than running it the moment the door closes. None of the EUHOMY or COMFEE' listings referenced in this guide mention a delay-start option, so if scheduling matters as much as noise itself, that's a real point in the Hamilton Beach machine's favor even though its 52 dB rating is the loudest published figure here.

A night cycle also interacts with your electricity bill, not just your neighbors — many utilities charge less per kilowatt-hour during off-peak overnight hours, and a delayed cycle is one of the few genuinely free ways to shave a little off a dishwasher's running cost. FAQ

Common questions about dishwasher noise

Are decibel ratings on portable dishwashers accurate?

Treat them as manufacturer claims rather than independently measured facts. Brands test in their own conditions, which they generally don't disclose, and there's no third-party audit requirement for a dishwasher's decibel rating the way there is for ENERGY STAR certification. The relative ranking between machines that do publish a figure — a 40 dB unit versus a 52 dB unit, for example — is still a useful signal, even if the exact number in your kitchen may differ.

What's a genuinely quiet decibel rating for a countertop dishwasher?

Around 40 dB is the quietest published figure common in this category, comparable to a quiet library or a refrigerator's hum from across the room. Most machines that publish a number at all sit somewhere between 40 and 55 dB, and the decibel scale is logarithmic, so a 10 dB gap reads as roughly double the perceived loudness rather than a small difference.

Does running a dishwasher at night disturb neighbors in an apartment?

It can, depending on your building's construction and how the machine is placed — sound and vibration travel differently through shared walls and floors than through open air. A delay-start function, where the machine supports one, lets you schedule the wash for a time when running noise matters less, and placing the machine away from a shared wall with a vibration-dampening mat underneath reduces what transmits through the structure.

Can I make a loud portable dishwasher quieter without buying a new one?

Placement changes what you actually hear more than most people expect. A dense mat or folded towel under the machine reduces vibration transmitted into the countertop, a straight and unkinked drain hose avoids the loudest part of the cycle — the end-of-wash pump — running harder than it needs to, and running only full loads means water strikes dishes instead of bare metal, which is quieter. None of these close a wide gap between a 40 dB and a 55 dB machine, but they narrow it.
