Hamilton Beach makes three portable dishwashers. Which one is actually right for you?

One name, one brand badge, and three machines that don't behave like siblings. A $268 countertop unit fills from its own tank. Two $600 machines roll on wheels and clip to a faucet — and differ from each other mainly in paint. Here's what the badge is actually covering.

Search "Hamilton Beach portable dishwasher" on Amazon and the results don't look like one product family. One listing is a compact box that costs $268 and fills from a six-liter water tank you pour in by hand. The other two are 19-inch-wide machines on wheels that cost roughly $600 each, connect to a kitchen faucet, and look — at a glance — like the same appliance photographed twice. They are not the same appliance photographed twice, but they are close enough to each other that the real decision in this lineup isn't "which class," the way it usually is in this category. It's "white or platinum," and then separately, "do I even want the class these two belong to."

That's the structure of this page. First we sort out why one brand name is attached to a $268 unit and two $600 units that have almost nothing in common with it. Then we take the two wheeled machines apart to find what, if anything, actually separates them beyond the finish. Then we land on a plain recommendation by kitchen type, because the three machines here solve three different problems and only one of your kitchens exists.

The badge

Why one brand name covers a $268 unit and two $600 machines

Hamilton Beach is a small-appliance brand first — coffee makers, blenders, slow cookers — and "portable dishwasher" is a newer line grafted onto that catalogue rather than a dedicated product family built from one platform. That's visible in how differently the three machines are built, not just how differently they're priced.

The Hamilton Beach Portable Countertop Dishwasher No Hookup Unit is built around a 6-liter water bank, sized and marketed for a tabletop, an apartment counter or an RV galley — the listing says so directly. It needs no faucet connection at all: fill the tank, set a delay timer if you want the cycle to finish later, and run a rapid-cleaning cycle. At $268.00, it's priced like the small appliance it is.

The other two — the 19-inch-wide white model at $599.99 and the stainless/platinum model at $585.62 — are a different class of object entirely: freestanding units on wheels, built to roll to the sink, hook onto the faucet and roll back when they're done. Both are rated for 8 place settings, the figure that puts a machine in range of a genuine built-in dishwasher rather than a countertop gadget. The $330-plus gap between the tank unit and either wheeled machine isn't Hamilton Beach charging more for the same thing in a bigger box — it's the gap between two different appliance categories that happen to share a logo.

None of that is unusual for this market; we've looked at the same pattern in countertop versus 18-inch portables generally, and in Silonn's own two-model lineup. What makes Hamilton Beach's version worth a dedicated page is that two of its three machines are close enough to each other that buyers reasonably wonder if they're paying twice for the same decision.

The 18-inch pair

The platinum and white wheeled models: what actually differs

Set the two wheeled listings side by side and the specifications that are published are nearly identical. Both are 19 inches wide. Both are rated for 8 place settings. Both are freestanding units on wheels, meant to be rolled to the sink, connected to the faucet, and rolled back against the wall between uses. The white model is titled "Portable Dishwasher with Wheels, 19in Wide, 8 Place Settings Capacity, White"; the other is titled simply "Portable Dishwasher, SS/Platinum" — stainless-steel-and-platinum finish standing in for a model name.

What the two listings do differ on, plainly, is finish and price. The white unit runs $599.99; the platinum/stainless unit runs $585.62 — a $7.29 difference that is not large enough to represent a different tier of machine. Beyond the color and that small price gap, our product data doesn't give us a documented difference in cycle count, noise rating or capacity between the two — which is the honest way to put it: we have no published spec that says one washes better, dries better or holds more than the other.

That absence of a documented difference matters more than it sounds. It means the decision between these two wheeled machines is almost entirely aesthetic — whether platinum/stainless or white fits the rest of your kitchen's hardware — rather than a performance trade-off like the countertop-versus-wheeled choice is. If your cabinet pulls and faucet are brushed steel, the platinum finish matches; if your kitchen leans white-and-light, the white unit does. Either way you're buying the same class of machine at within $10 of the same price.

One caution applies to both: as with any 18-to-19-inch wheeled portable, confirm your faucet has a removable aerator before ordering either one. Pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets generally can't take the adapter these machines rely on, and that single fact rules out the entire wheeled pair for a meaningful share of American kitchens — a problem the tank-fed countertop unit, discussed next, doesn't have.

The odd one out

The countertop tank model is a different class, not a smaller version of the big one

It's tempting to read the $268 countertop unit as "the small Hamilton Beach dishwasher" and the two $600 machines as "the full-size ones" — a single product line scaled up or down. The specs don't support that reading. The countertop unit isn't a shrunken 8-place-setting machine; it's built around a completely different water strategy, and that strategy is the entire point of buying it.

The 6-liter water bank means this unit needs nothing but a power outlet. No faucet, no adapter threading, no aerator compatibility to check, no hose reaching across a counter to the sink. That's precisely why the listing names tabletop use, apartments and RVs as its target settings — all three are places where a faucet connection is either impossible (an RV galley, a tabletop with no sink nearby) or politically complicated (a rental where you don't want to touch the plumbing). Delay timer and rapid cleaning round out a feature set built for convenience in a small footprint, not for washing a large family's daily load.

The wheeled pair, by contrast, exists to solve the opposite problem: a kitchen with a workable faucet but no built-in dishwasher cabinet, where the buyer wants something closer to a real appliance's capacity. Comparing the tank unit's 6-liter bank against the wheeled machines' 8 place settings isn't comparing two sizes of the same thing — it's comparing a fill-and-run convenience appliance against a faucet-dependent machine built for volume. Choosing between them isn't a step up or down a product ladder; it's a choice between two different answers to "how does water get into this machine," which is exactly the question we walk through in general terms in our water-level window guide and our piece on running small loads efficiently.

Money

The price gap within one brand, and what it buys

Lay the three prices next to each other and the gap is stark: $268.00 for the tank unit, $599.99 for the white wheeled machine, $585.62 for the platinum one. That's better than double from the cheapest Hamilton Beach dishwasher to either of the other two — and almost nothing between the two expensive ones.

  • From $268 to roughly $600 you're not buying "more dishwasher" in a simple sense — you're buying freedom from the tank. The 6-liter bank caps how much water a single cycle can use; an 8-place-setting faucet-fed machine draws as much as the cycle needs and never requires a hand refill.
  • From $268 to roughly $600 you're also buying rated capacity: 8 place settings against a tank unit whose listing doesn't quote a place-setting figure at all, because its water bank — not a plate count — is the headline spec.
  • From $599.99 to $585.62 — the gap between the two wheeled machines — you're buying almost nothing documented beyond finish. $7.29 is not a meaningful price signal for a performance difference; treat it as the cost of choosing platinum over white, or vice versa.

Put differently: the big, consequential price jump in this lineup happens once, between the tank unit and either wheeled machine, and it tracks a real change in what the appliance can do. The small jump between the two wheeled machines tracks nothing but paint. Don't let a $600 price tag talk you into assuming the platinum unit out-cleans the white one — our product data gives no basis for that claim, and we won't invent one.

The decision

Which Hamilton Beach model fits which kitchen

Buy the countertop tank unit if

Any one of these is true

You're in an RV, a dorm, or an apartment where you either can't or don't want to touch the faucet. Or your kitchen's tap is a pull-down or sprayer style that won't take an adapter. Or your budget stops well under $300. Or you're washing for one or two people and don't need 8-place-setting volume. Any one of these settles it in favor of the $268 unit — it's the only Hamilton Beach machine that asks nothing of your plumbing.

Buy one of the wheeled machines if

All of these are true

A straight kitchen faucet with a standard, removable aerator; roughly 19 inches of floor space with a clear path to the sink; enough people in the household that 8 place settings matters; around $600 to spend. Once those are true, the only remaining question is white or platinum — pick by what matches the rest of your kitchen's hardware, since the documented specs don't give you another reason to choose.

If you're torn between the two wheeled finishes

Don't overthink it, and don't let the $7.29 gap suggest a capability difference that our product data doesn't show. Stand in your kitchen and look at what's already stainless or already white — the fridge, the range hood, the faucet itself — and match the dishwasher to that, the same way you'd pick a toaster finish. If nothing in the room leans either way, the white model is $7.29 cheaper, which is as good a tiebreaker as any.

And if neither wheeled machine's faucet requirement works for your kitchen, don't force it — go back to the tank-fed unit rather than assuming a $600 price tag guarantees a good fit. FAQ

Common questions about the Hamilton Beach lineup

Are the white and platinum Hamilton Beach wheeled dishwashers actually different machines?

Based on the published listings, no meaningful difference beyond finish and a small price gap. Both are 19 inches wide, both are rated for 8 place settings, both are freestanding on wheels and connect to a kitchen faucet. The white model runs $599.99 and the platinum/stainless model runs $585.62 — a $7.29 difference that reads as a finish premium rather than a documented performance gap. We have no published spec showing one washes, dries or holds more than the other.

Why is the countertop Hamilton Beach dishwasher so much cheaper than the wheeled ones?

It's a different class of appliance, not a smaller version of the same machine. The countertop unit fills from a 6-liter water bank and needs only a power outlet — no faucet, no adapter, no hose to the sink. The wheeled machines are faucet-fed, rated for 8 place settings, and built to approach a built-in dishwasher's capacity. The roughly $330 gap tracks that real difference in what each machine can do, not a markup on an identical product.

Can the Hamilton Beach countertop dishwasher work without any faucet connection?

Yes — that's the entire design premise. It's marketed specifically for tabletop use, apartments and RVs because its 6-liter water bank replaces a faucet connection entirely. Fill the tank by hand, set the delay timer if needed, and run the cycle. This is also the Hamilton Beach model to choose if your kitchen's faucet is a pull-down or sprayer style that can't accept the adapter the wheeled machines need.

Do the wheeled Hamilton Beach dishwashers need a special kind of faucet?

They need a straight kitchen faucet with a removable aerator and standard threads, which is typical of older, non-sprayer taps. Pull-down and pull-out sprayer faucets — common in many modern kitchens and rentals — generally can't accept the adapter these machines rely on. If that describes your faucet, the countertop tank unit is the only Hamilton Beach dishwasher that will work in your kitchen.
