The mechanism

What ENERGY STAR actually measures

ENERGY STAR is a joint program of the EPA and the Department of Energy, not a manufacturer-run scheme, and a dishwasher doesn't earn the mark by printing it on the box. The manufacturer submits the model to an EPA-recognized lab, which runs it through a standardized test cycle under fixed conditions — a defined water hardness, a defined incoming water temperature, a defined soil load — and records exactly two outputs: how much energy the cycle consumed and how much water it used. Certification is a pass or fail against a ceiling set below the federal minimum efficiency standard for that class of machine. Miss the ceiling and the badge doesn't appear, no matter what the listing copy claims elsewhere.

Two things about that process are easy to miss. First, the ceiling is set separately for different classes of dishwasher — a full-size 24-inch built-in isn't held to the same numeric limit as a compact countertop model washing four to six place settings, because the smaller machine's baseline water and energy use is different to begin with. A portable or countertop machine carrying the mark has cleared the ceiling built for its own size class, not the one built for the dishwasher under your neighbor's counter. Second, the test measures water and energy consumption only. It says nothing about how well the machine gets dried egg off a plate, how loud it runs, or how long it lasts — noise in particular is a separate question we cover on its own terms in the quietest portable dishwashers.

We don't have the current numeric thresholds this narrower countertop and portable class is tested against, and printing a number we can't verify would be worse than not printing one — the criteria are revised periodically and a stale figure reads as current when it isn't. What's reliable, and unlikely to change, is the shape of the test: a fixed lab cycle, an independent lab, a published ceiling, and a badge that appears only if the machine comes in under it.

The water number

Water per cycle, against a hand-washed sink

The EPA's own published comparison for the ENERGY STAR dishwasher program puts hand-washing a full sink of dishes at up to 27 gallons of water — the tap running while you scrub, rinse and set pieces aside, which is closer to how most people actually wash than a careful measured trickle. A certified dishwasher cycle, by contrast, is capped under 4 gallons. That's roughly an 85 percent cut in water per load, and it's the number the certification is built to guarantee, not a figure any single manufacturer invented for its own listing.

Of the five machines in this guide, only the GE Profile countertop model states its own figure directly in the listing: it says the machine "reduces water use by over 75% when compared to handwashing," which lines up with the EPA's range and is the kind of claim we're comfortable repeating because it's attributed to the manufacturer's own published bullet, not our estimate dressed up as theirs. COMFEE', Midea, SPT and BLACK+DECKER don't quote a specific gallons-per-cycle or percentage figure for the models here. All five carry the ENERGY STAR mark, so all five have cleared the same water-use ceiling in testing — but we're not going to invent a matching percentage for machines whose listings don't state one. Treat "roughly 75 to 85 percent less water than washing by hand" as the honest range for the category, not an exact figure for every unit on this page.

One caveat worth stating plainly: none of this accounts for how carefully you hand-wash. Someone who fills a basin and shuts off the tap between rinses uses far less than 27 gallons; someone who lets the hot water run continuously while scrubbing can easily use more. The dishwasher's number is fixed by the test cycle. The hand-washing number is not fixed by anything, which is exactly why the comparison favors a machine — it turns a variable habit into a repeatable one.

The energy number

Power draw, and where it goes

None of the five machines in this guide connects to your home's hot-water line — they take in water from the faucet and heat it internally with their own element. That single design fact decides where the electricity goes: the heater that raises incoming water to wash and rinse temperature is by far the largest load in the cycle, not the pump that drives the spray arms or the control board that runs the timer. A motor moving water around a small tub for the length of a cycle draws comparatively little; an element bringing several liters of water up to a target temperature is where most of the kilowatt-hours go.

This is also why cycle selection changes the electricity bill more than it changes the water bill. A Heavy or Baby-Care program that targets a higher temperature and holds it longer runs the heating element for more of the cycle than a Rapid or Glass program does, even though the pump behaves almost identically either way. We go through why these cycles run as long as they do — and which ones are safe to skip on a lightly soiled load — in why a portable dishwasher cycle takes two hours. The short version for this guide: shorter, cooler cycles use less electricity, not just less time.

The incoming water temperature matters too, and it's one variable the ENERGY STAR test standardizes that your kitchen doesn't. A machine hooked to a cold-water-only line has to do more heating work, cycle after cycle, than the same machine on a line that runs warm. That's outside anything a spec sheet or a certification can capture, and it's a real reason two households running the identical model can see different electricity draws from it.

The math

The yearly saving, in real dollars

Here's a worked estimate, with every assumption stated so you can swap in your own numbers — water and sewer rates vary by a factor of five or more between cities, and this math is only as good as the rate you plug into it.

Assume a two-to-three-person household that runs the dishwasher five times a week: 260 cycles a year. Using the EPA's hand-wash figure of up to 27 gallons a load against a certified cycle capped under 4 gallons, that's roughly 23 gallons saved per cycle, or about 5,980 gallons over the year. Combined water-and-sewer rates run anywhere from around $4 to $20 per 1,000 gallons depending on the utility and the state — we'll use $8 per 1,000 gallons as a deliberately middle-of-the-road figure, which puts the water saving at roughly $48 a year against washing the same load by hand. Check your own bill for the actual combined rate; ours is a placeholder, not a national average we're claiming to know precisely.

The electricity side is harder to put an honest number on, and we're not going to fake one. None of the five listings here publishes a kWh-per-cycle or kWh-per-year figure, which is the number you'd need to calculate an electricity cost with any confidence — draw varies by cycle, incoming water temperature and how full the tub is, and a plug-in watt meter on your own machine will tell you more than a spec sheet ever could. What we can say honestly: the ENERGY STAR ceiling exists specifically to keep that number below what an equivalent uncertified machine in the same size class would draw, so there is a second saving here on top of the water figure above — we just can't attach a verified dollar amount to it without a meter reading, and won't pretend otherwise.

So the defensible number from this page is water alone: on the assumptions above, roughly $48 in the first year, growing slightly as utility rates rise, plus an unquantified electricity saving on top of it. Against a $310 to $610 purchase price, that's not a fast payback if you're comparing to hand-washing outright — it's a modest, compounding saving on a machine you were likely buying anyway for the convenience, with certification tipping the water math in your favor rather than justifying the purchase by itself.

The other side

When a cheaper uncertified unit still wins

Certification is a real, verified saving on the numbers above — but it's a small one relative to the price of the appliance, and there are ordinary situations where chasing the badge is the wrong priority.

You run the machine rarely. The yearly saving above assumes five cycles a week. A single person running the dishwasher twice a week is looking at closer to $19 a year in water savings on the same assumptions — a real number, but not one that should outrank capacity, price or whether the machine fits your kitchen at all.

Your local water and power rates are low. Swap our $8-per-1,000-gallon placeholder for a cheaper utility's actual $4 rate and the water saving above is cut in half. In some regions the arithmetic in this guide simply produces a smaller number than it does elsewhere, and no certification changes that.

Fit beats certification. A certified machine that's too tall for your wall cabinets, or faucet-only when your kitchen has a pull-out sprayer tap with no removable aerator, is worse than an uncertified one that actually works. Measure the clearance and check your faucet before you filter by badge — the tank-fed models we cover separately sidestep the faucet question entirely for kitchens where it's the dealbreaker.

You're really comparing it to a built-in, not to hand-washing. If you have a dishwasher cabinet, plumbing and permission to use them, a portable machine — certified or not — usually loses to a proper built-in on capacity and water use per place setting. What you give up moving from a built-in to a portable is a separate trade-off from certification, and we cover it directly in portable vs. built-in: what you give up.

None of that is an argument against the five machines above — all are competitive on their own specs, certification aside, which is why they're in this guide. It's an argument for putting certification third or fourth on your list of filters, after capacity, water source and physical fit, rather than first.

FAQ

Common questions

What does ENERGY STAR certification actually test on a dishwasher?

An independent, EPA-recognized lab runs the machine through a standardized cycle under fixed conditions and measures two things: energy consumed and water used. The model passes only if both come in under a ceiling set for its size class, below the federal minimum efficiency standard. It doesn't test cleaning performance, noise or durability.

How much water does hand-washing use compared to a certified dishwasher?

The EPA's published comparison puts hand-washing a full sink at up to 27 gallons of water, against a certified dishwasher cycle capped under 4 gallons — roughly an 85 percent reduction. How carefully you hand-wash changes the real-world gap; the dishwasher's number is fixed by the test, yours isn't.

Do all five machines in this guide save the same amount of water and energy?

All five have cleared the same ENERGY STAR ceiling for their size class, but only the GE Profile states its own water-reduction figure in the listing, over 75% versus handwashing. We haven't repeated that percentage for the other four, since their listings don't publish one — treat 75 to 85 percent as the honest range for the category rather than an exact figure for every unit.

Is it worth paying more for an ENERGY STAR portable dishwasher?

On the water savings alone, the payback is modest — around $48 a year on our stated assumptions for a household running five cycles a week, less for lighter use or cheaper local utility rates. It's a real, compounding saving rather than a fast one, and it shouldn't outrank capacity, water source or whether the machine physically fits your kitchen.
